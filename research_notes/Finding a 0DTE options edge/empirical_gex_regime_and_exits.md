## Q1. Does the dealer-gamma regime (spot vs the prior day's gamma flip) predict intraday continuation vs mean reversion, realized range, or 1-minute reversal rates?

### Takeaway
No. On 1,016 ticker-days the PM-on-AM and last-30-on-prior-30 slopes are indistinguishable from zero in both regimes and in every distance-to-flip tercile (all CIs straddle zero, sign-agreement 50–53 %), and the regime split even flips sign between the in-sample and out-of-sample halves. The only regime differences that reach nominal significance are small volatility effects (positive-gamma days have a 0.11 pp narrower range and a 0.5 pp higher 1-minute reversal rate), and neither survives a multiple-testing correction.

### Inferences
- The Baltussen-style prediction (positive gamma → intraday mean reversion, negative gamma → continuation) does not show up in these ETFs at the daily half-day or 30-minute horizon using UW's prior-day flip; the POS−NEG slope difference is +0.03 with a CI of ±0.15, and the IS/OOS sign reversal says the point estimates are noise.
- The two nominally significant effects are volatility, not direction: NEG days have ~0.11 pp more range (1.30 vs 1.19 %) and slightly fewer 1-minute reversals. Even if real, a 0.1 pp range difference cannot be monetised with long 0DTE options whose round-trip cost is 3–6 % of premium (see Q4).
- Multiple-testing note: Q1 reports 27 groups × 2 regressions × 3 statistics = 162 interval tests plus 35 difference tests (~197 CIs). At a 5 % nominal level ~10 false rejections are expected; 4 were observed. The Bonferroni threshold is p ≈ 0.00025 and nothing in Q1 approaches it.
- Plain verdict: the prior-day gamma-flip regime is not a usable momentum/mean-reversion filter for SPY/QQQ/IWM intraday trades.

### Gaps
- SPX index bars are unavailable in this environment, so the test could not be run on the index the dealer-gamma literature usually studies.
- The gex-levels endpoint reports only one flip per day (`source: vol`); UW's documentation of how `vol` differs from an OI-based flip was not available offline, so the regime is whatever UW publishes.

## Q2. Does UW's 1-minute spot-exposure data (net gamma OI/vol/dir, charm, vanna) predict the next 30/60-minute signed or absolute SPY return?

### Takeaway
Signed returns: no (pooled Spearman ICs −0.06 for gamma_oi with CIs covering zero; the 30-minute change in volume-gamma has an IC of −0.06 to −0.10 whose CI barely excludes zero, one of 56 ICs tested). Absolute returns: yes, negative intraday net gamma coincides with larger moves (IC −0.21 [−0.30, −0.12]; mean |30-min return| 12.6 bp when gamma_oi < 0 vs 8.7 bp when > 0), partly surviving a control for trailing realized volatility, but this is a volatility-regime fact, not a direction signal. The intraday sign agrees with the prior-day flip regime only 54 % of the time.

### Inferences
- Net intraday dealer gamma (OI or volume based) is a reasonable concurrent volatility gauge (negative gamma ≈ 45 % larger 30–60-minute absolute moves, and about half of that is between-day, i.e. "high-vol days are negative-gamma days"). It adds a little beyond trailing realized volatility (partial IC −0.17) but it says nothing about direction.
- The 30-minute change in volume-gamma has the largest signed IC (−0.06 to −0.10, falling gamma → slightly positive next return). With 56 IC tests, ~3 CIs excluding zero are expected by chance; this one is marginal, its within-day statistic is confounded, and a 1.7 bp mean difference in the underlying is ≈ $5 of option P&L on an ATM SPY 0DTE, roughly the round-trip cost, before any slippage from acting on it. It is not an edge on its own.
- The intraday gamma sign and the prior-day flip regime are nearly unrelated (54 % agreement), so "the regime" is not a single well-defined object in UW's data; any strategy that keys off one definition would not key off the other.
- Plain verdict: nothing in the 1-minute exposure feed predicts the sign of the next 30/60-minute SPY move; negative gamma predicts bigger moves, which for a long-premium trader is a vol-regime filter at best.

### Gaps
- Only SPY and 60 sessions were fetched (per the brief); QQQ/IWM exposure series and the strike-level endpoints were not tested.
- UW's units and the exact construction of the `_dir` (directional) variant are undocumented in the payload; no field definitions beyond names were available offline.

## Q3. When price first comes within 0.1 % of the prior-day call wall, put wall or gamma magnet, what happens next, and does price pin to the level into the 15:55 close?

### Takeaway
Nothing distinguishable from distance-matched random levels: next-30/60-minute returns after a first touch are within a few basis points of zero for all three levels, the magnet shows zero pinning (34.5 % of touched days close within 0.25 % vs 34.6 % for random levels), and the put wall is if anything anti-pinning (−6.3 pp [−12.2, −1.2]). The one directional result with a CI excluding zero (put wall touched from below: −9.4 bp [−15.8, −3.0] over the next 30 minutes, n = 103) is one of ~50 comparisons.

### Inferences
- UW's prior-day walls and magnet behave like arbitrary price levels at the same distance from the open: same touch rates, same forward returns, same closing distances. The magnet "pin" effect that the strategy's `avoid_pin` gate assumes is absent at the 0.25 % and 0.5 % tolerances.
- The put-wall-from-below rejection (−9 bp in 30 minutes, n = 103) is the only candidate effect; it would need an independent sample and, at 9 bp of underlying move, is about one round-trip cost for an ATM 0DTE put, so it is not tradeable even if real.
- Multiple-testing note: Q3 reports ~51 CIs (3 levels × {pooled, from below, from above} × {through30, through60, pin25, |close_rel|} plus 15 difference tests); 2–3 false rejections are expected at 5 % and 2 were found.
- Plain verdict: no pin, no magnet, no wall effect beyond chance in this sample.

### Gaps
- Levels are integers, so the 0.1 % tolerance (≈ $0.65 on SPY) is comparable to the level's own rounding; a strike-level GEX profile would be needed to test "the nearest high-gamma strike" rather than UW's single published number.
- Only the prior-day snapshot was tested; same-day intraday walls (which UW updates during the session) were not fetched.

## Q4. Which exit-engine configuration maximises the cost-adjusted expectancy of the simulated long 0DTE trades, in-sample and out-of-sample, and is the improvement about the entries or just the option price path?

### Takeaway
Across all 576 configurations (stop × breakeven × guardian trail × partial take × time stop), none has a positive expectancy under the spread cost model on the sweep entries, the continuation entries, the pooled set, or 3,000 random entries. The best configuration (stop −60 %, breakeven at +6 %, 25 % give-back trail armed at +15 %, no partial take, no time stop) lifts the pooled expectancy from −$3.84 to −$1.78 per contract [−2.80, −0.73] but does exactly the same thing for random entries (−$1.34 [−2.23, −0.46]); the gain is a property of the option price path, not the entries, and no configuration beats random entries.

### Inferences
- The grid orders cleanly: tight early trailing (25 % give-back once +15 % is reached) > looser trails; early breakeven (+6 %) > none; no partial take > partial; the initial stop width and the time stop barely matter because almost every trade exits by breakeven/trail within 5–15 minutes. These preferences are identical on random entries, so they are facts about 0DTE option price paths (fat right tail, early reversals), not about the signals.
- Cost hurdle: the best gross (mid) expectancy any exit engine can extract is about +$2.4–2.5 per contract (1.5 % of premium) on either entry set, whereas the realistic round trip is $3.5–4.6 (3–6 % of premium). The engine would need roughly double its gross edge, or costs would need to halve, for the expectancy to reach zero.
- Multiple-testing note: 576 configurations × 4 entry sets × 3 cost models were examined; because the best spread-model expectancy is negative everywhere, selection bias can only make the true picture worse, and the IS-selected configurations lose out-of-sample in every set.
- Plain verdict: exit engineering cannot rescue these entries; the "best" engine is a slightly less bad way of losing the spread.

### Gaps
- Breakeven and trail fills are assumed at the exact stop level (as in `exits.py`); real stop fills on 0DTE options are typically worse, so the spread model is optimistic for the breakeven/trail-heavy configurations (the adverse model, −$7 to −$10 at best, brackets this).
- The random-entry contracts are limited to strikes present in the cache (strikes that were ATM at some signal time), a mild selection toward strikes near the day's traded range.
