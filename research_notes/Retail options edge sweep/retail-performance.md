# Retail option traders: the empirical base rate (sweep of 2026-10-14)

Scope: what identified retail option traders actually earn after costs, in which contracts, and why; what payment for order flow (PFOF) and price improvement do to the cost side; what fraction of trades and traders lose; and what the evidence says about alert services, newsletters and social-media trading calls. The purpose is a number: the hurdle a retail option strategy must clear before any entry rule is worth testing. Grades: **[PR]** peer-reviewed, **[WP]** working paper (SSRN/NBER/university or regulator staff paper), **[EX]** exchange or regulator publication, **[PRAC]** practitioner white paper or trade press, **[VEND]** vendor marketing (not evidence; listed only to flag it), **[SEC-SUM]** a number taken from a secondary summary because the primary text could not be retrieved (stated each time).

Access notes. SSRN abstract pages returned HTTP 403 throughout. Full texts were read from mirrors and converted with `pdftotext`: Bryzgalova–Pavlova–Sikorskaya (LBS repository, the published *Journal of Finance* typescript), de Silva–Smith–So (MIT IDE, 4 March 2024 draft), Bauer–Cosemans–Eichholtz (Netspar working-paper version, 2007, of the 2009 *JBF* article), Ernst–Spatt (NBER w29883 revised), Hendershott–Khan–Riordan (Haas mirror of the *RFS* 2026 typescript), Bogousslavsky–Muravyev (LSU mirror), the SEC staff report of October 2021, Robinhood's Rule 606 report for Q2 2025 (FINRA host), the CCMR staff report of June 2025, and the Huang–Schwarz comment letter to the SEC of April 2026. Han–Lee–Liu (Taiwan) and SEBI's September 2024 study are cited from secondary reproductions and marked as such. No paid API or credential was used.

## 1. The short answer: what a retail option strategy must overcome

The literature agrees on the sign, the mechanism and, within a factor of two, the size.

Stated plainly: a retail buyer of short-dated options starts each trade 5–13 % of premium behind on the spread (marketable orders at a PFOF broker), loses roughly 0.5–1 % of premium per day to theta on a near-the-money contract (100 % by the close on 0DTE), and brings no directional information on average. The aggregate retail portfolio is positive *gross* (Bryzgalova: +$10–13 m/day) and negative *net*: the loss is the cost, not the forecast. Sellers of the same contracts are positive net but their edge is roughly a third of what buyers lose and is not statistically distinguishable from zero beyond the intraday horizon (Bryzgalova Table VI; Beckmeyer Table 2). Nothing in the peer-reviewed or working-paper record shows a retail-accessible long-option strategy with a positive expectation after the quoted spread; the only positive net cells are option *writing* and limit-order liquidity provision, and both are small.

## 2. Who trades, and how much

**Volume.** OCC average daily volume 19.6 m contracts (Dec 2019) → > 30 m (Q1 2020) → 34.3 m (Dec 2020) [SEC staff report, Oct 2021, fn. 52, EX]; 29.5 m/day in 2020, 38.6 m/day in 2021 to 15 Oct, "almost 100 percent higher than the 19.8 million contracts per day traded in 2019" [FINRA Regulatory Notice 22-08, March 2022, EX]. The share of volume from one-contract trades on the 50 most-traded names rose from 10 % at end-2019 to 14 % by mid-2020; the average electronic auction shrank from ≈ 9 to 6 contracts while the number of auctions per day rose ≈ 134 % [SEC, EX].

**Retail share.** Bryzgalova, Pavlova and Sikorskaya [PR, *JF* 78(6) 2023, 3465–3514; OPRA transaction data Nov 2019–Jun 2021, ETF and equity options, index options excluded] divide each broker's monthly Rule 606 PFOF by the average payment per contract to get the wholesaler-intermediated volume: "Retail trading accounts for 62.6% of the total market volume." Their direct measure, single-leg price-improvement-auction trades (OPRA code SLAN, "SLIM"), captures 58–84 % of the market and marketable-limit volume implied by Rule 606; SLIM dollar volume grew 101 % from January 2020 to July 2021 against 158 % growth in options PFOF; the average SLIM share of volume exceeded 20 % in summer 2020. Hendershott–Khan–Riordan [PR] cite the same paper for "more than 95% of retail options orders purchased by wholesalers." Cboe's LiveVol-based estimate is that retail brokers "have made up between 55% and 60% of the options market since the beginning of last year" (Catherine Clay, Cboe, via FOW, Aug 2025) [EX/PRAC]; Cboe Insights (Mandy Xu, 2 June 2025) puts retail at 54 % of SPX 0DTE volume in May 2025 against a low of 47 % in April, with 0DTE at 61 % of SPX volume [EX]. Identified retail in Beckmeyer et al. (auction codes, ≤ 10 contracts) is only 2–4 % of SPX volume and > 6 % of 0DTE, which Amaya et al. call implausibly low because retail limit orders never reach the auction (companion note §2).

**What retail trades.** Bryzgalova Table I (SLIM vs non-SLIM, frequency-weighted): calls 71.5 % of trades (69.4 % of volume) vs 64.2 % for non-SLIM; one-contract trades 46.2 % of trades (6.6 % of volume); trades under $250 are 41.6 % of trades and carry a 23.5 % quoted / 11.6 % effective spread; 49.9 % of SLIM volume is in options with less than a week to expiry (41.5 % for non-SLIM); written options are slightly more frequent than purchased (49.6 % vs 46.6 % of trades). Weekly ATM options carry implied leverage of 58–72 (fn. 19). Bogousslavsky–Muravyev [WP; 5,182 traders at retail brokers, 2.4 m parent trades of which 0.9 m options, ≈ $15 bn, 2020–2022]: option trades are over a third of all trades; the median option maturity fell from four days (2020) to one day (2022); 0DTE were 23 % of option trades in 2022; 68.8 % of option trades are closed the same day; median holding 0.54 hours, mean 82.8 hours; average option trade $2,006 (median $337); the median option trade is on a $262 underlying (ex-SPX) against an $8 stock for the median stock trade. de Silva–Smith–So [PR]: in 2020 retail accounted for more than $250 bn of single-name option volume; around earnings retail "primarily purchase at-the-money options." Lakonishok, Lee, Pearson and Poteshman [PR, *RFS* 2007; CBOE 1990–2001]: non-market-maker investors hold about four times as much long-call as long-put open interest; the least sophisticated group sharply increased call purchases on growth (not value) stocks in the bubble. Mahani–Poteshman [PR, *J. Empirical Finance* 15(4) 2008]: unsophisticated investors enter option positions that load on growth stocks before earnings announcements although value stocks outperform growth "by a wide margin" at those announcements. Bauer et al. [PR]: 80 % of surveyed clients use options "primarily for speculation"; 10 % use the Greeks.

## 3. What retail earns: the studies, with their numbers

### 3.1 Bryzgalova, Pavlova, Sikorskaya (2023) [PR] — the aggregate U.S. record

Data: OPRA trades with the SLAN flag, Nov 2019–Jun 2021, ETF and equity options (index excluded); OptionMetrics closes; Rule 606 PFOF reports. Performance = direction × size × 100 × (price at t+h − price at t); gross from midquotes, net from the actual fill price to the closing midquote (exit costs ignored, so net is an upper bound; the authors say so).

Table VI, mean daily P&L of the retail (buy-minus-sell) portfolio, $ m: gross +10.46 (intraday), +11.86 (1 d), +10.91 (2 d), +10.59 (5 d), +10.36 (10 d), +12.90 (to expiry); net **−4.92, −3.52, −4.47, −4.80, −5.03** (t = −11.2 to −2.6) and −1.6 to expiry (t −0.3). Buys net −8.71 (intraday) to −12.74 (10 d); sells net +3.79 (intraday, t 2.4) to +7.71 (10 d, t 0.3): "positive even net of the observed transaction costs, although, with the exception of intraday performance, not statistically different from zero." Calls net −3.48 → −4.87/day; puts −1.45 → −0.16. At-the-money contracts carry most of the loss (net −3.17 → −4.69/day); contracts under a week to expiry lose at every horizon (net −2.14 → −3.09/day); 1–3 month and 3–12 month contracts are net zero at 5–10 days. Aggregate: "Under the assumption of a 10-day holding period, retail investors lost $2.10 billion"; the aggregate distance of fills from mid is $6.4 bn against ≈ $0.9 bn of commissions. Worst months: Jan 2021 −$672 m, Feb 2021 −$358 m, Dec 2020 −$321 m; the 100 most WSB-mentioned names account for > 50 % of losses; retail gained on NVDA/AAPL/MRNA and lost on GME/AMC/TSLA/AMZN. Per-dollar, no-leverage: **−28 to −40 bp per $100 traded** at 1–10 days; to expiry +37 bp, not significant. To-expiration P&L flips sign (shorts lose, longs gain) through 3–12 month contracts in a 33–38 % up-market; delta-hedged performance shows no flip. Spreads: full-sample SLIM quoted 13.7 % / effective 6.7 %; < 1 week 12.6 % / 6.6 %; slightly OTM 29 %; Muravyev–Pearson's 17.2 % average quoted spread on S&P 500 stock options against 3.55 bp on the stocks (fn. 6). Two populations: "those who buy short-term (weekly) options and lose money and (ii) those who sell these options and earn a premium most of the time."

What it does and does not say. It is the only peer-reviewed estimate of aggregate retail option P&L in the U.S. It prices marketable orders only, so it overstates costs for limit-order users (the authors show All-Internalized and All-Retail proxies are statistically no different in profitability). Amaya et al. [WP, Cboe-funded] re-measure Cboe/EDGX SLIM trades with exchange-side directions and find customer trades indistinguishable from zero (companion note §1).

### 3.2 de Silva, Smith, So — "Losing is Optional" [PR, *Review of Finance* 30(2), 2026, 489–535; draft read: MIT IDE, 4 March 2024]

Data: Nasdaq NOTO and PHLX PHOTO end-of-day files, contract-day buy/sell volume by clientele, ≈ 25 % of market-wide option volume, 1 Jan 2010–28 Feb 2021; retail = the non-professional customer category (which the authors note may include small non-retail accounts, so retail losses are "likely understated"). Findings (introduction, pp. 4–6): retail buys options "in a concentrated fashion" before earnings announcements, most in those with high expected abnormal volatility (EAV), and buys ATM; for announcements in the top quintile of retail purchases, implied variances rise "roughly 40% more" into the event. Three loss sources: (1) ATM straddles underperform on high-EAV announcement dates relative to low-EAV dates by **11 %** (t = 19.55): retail overpays relative to realized volatility; (2) assuming retail holds to expiry and pays only the half-spread, high-EAV buyers lose "an average additional **9 %** of their investment"; (3) retail holds after the event (a disposition effect), and straddles underperform a further **9 %** over the following two weeks (t = 8.26). Total: "retail investors lose 5-to-9% of option investments around earnings announcements on average, and 10-to-14% for high EAV announcements … approximately $3 billion … Market makers are the primary beneficiaries." The authors benchmark against Bauer et al.: Dutch retail "lose an average of 1.81% per month on their option positions." The earlier (Dec 2022) draft, as summarised by Quantpedia [PRAC], put the aggregate at $1.5 bn and the full spread at "∼ 20 % of the option price"; the 2024 draft and the published abstract carry the $3 bn and 5–9 % / 10–14 % figures. Registry cross-reference: S08 finds no post-earnings drift to buy in 2024–26 and "sell the beat" adjusted −31 to −86 bp; S13/S13-R find a pre-earnings run-up of +20 bp adjusted carried by the tail; the IV ramp de Silva documents is the half of A5.2 that S11 is to measure.

### 3.3 Bauer, Cosemans, Eichholtz (2009) [PR, *J. Banking & Finance* 33(4), 731–746; numbers from the Netspar working-paper version, 2007]

68,146 accounts at a Dutch online discount broker, January 2000–March 2006, > 2 m monthly portfolio snapshots, 9 m trades of which 4.3 m (48 %) are option trades and 0.5 m futures; > 33 m option contracts = 7.2 % of all contracts traded on the Amsterdam options exchange in the period; 26,266 investors (38.5 %) traded options at least once. The average investor earns −1.14 % per month gross and **−1.76 % per month net** (p < 0.05); net Carhart alpha −1.20 %/month, Agarwal −1.10 % (p < 0.01). Gross alphas for non-derivatives traders are close to zero; "derivatives investors underperform non-derivatives traders by more than 3.5% a month" net over the full sample, and by 3.24 %/month gross in 2003–06. The mechanism is timing: after the 2000–02 crash option traders' "Hausse-Baisse" ratio (bullish ÷ bearish short-dated positions) stayed below one through 2003–04 while the market recovered, so "derivatives traders speculated on a further decline of the market" and missed the rebound. Published abstract: "Most investors incur substantial losses on their option investments, which are much larger than the losses from equity trading … poor market timing that results from overreaction to past stock market returns. High trading costs further contribute … Gambling and entertainment appear to be the most important motivations … strong evidence of performance persistence among option traders." Option traders are 2.5 years older and hold larger accounts than other investors — not small gambling accounts. Commission structure matters: a per-contract fee on many small contracts makes the relative cost of options higher than stocks.

### 3.4 Bogousslavsky, Muravyev — "An Anatomy of Retail Option Trading" [WP, 29 Aug 2024]

Trade-journal data (verified broker imports; retail brokers only, TD Ameritrade the largest), 2020–2022. Average option trade return **−0.93 %** (t −3.57), "comparable to typical option trading commissions" (TD's $0.65/contract ≈ 0.4 % of a $2,006, 6-contract trade) and "much smaller than typical option bid-ask spreads, which range from 5% to 10% or more. Possibly, retail traders use limit orders." Purchases **−3.95 %**; naked sales (13 % of trades) **+20 %** (+28 % incremental with date and trader fixed effects); index/ETF option trades **−3 %** vs single-stock +0.15 %; 0DTE **−4.7 %** relative to other trades (t −10), −2.95 % with controls, non-0DTE +0.19 %; no difference between calls and puts or across brokers; active traders (> 500 trades) +0.27 %/trade. The authors treat this as evidence that "concerns regarding substantial retail losses in options may be somewhat overstated" relative to the 3–9 %-per-trade losses of the proxy studies; the difference is the order type (limit vs marketable), the trader population (journal users are active and relatively sophisticated) and endogenous holding periods. Dollar P&L distribution: 10th/90th percentiles −$296/+$217; realized skewness of option trades negative, not the lottery payoff retail is assumed to seek.

### 3.5 Beckmeyer, Branger, Gayda [WP] and Fu, Li, Musto, Pearson [WP, SEC DERA] — SPX 0DTE

Companion note §1 has the tables. For the base rate: identified retail (≤ 10 contracts, auction-coded) lose −$241 k per day net on SPX 0DTE over Feb 2021–Sep 2023 (−$350 k/day after daily expirations began in May 2022), debit orders −$364 k/day and credit orders +$122 k/day; a single long put loses $16.3 on average (median −$35), a long call $20.7; margin-adjusted means −5.6 % / −9.4 %, medians −0.7 % / −0.8 %; more than 25 % of single-leg trades expire at −100 %, the top 5 % return > 178 %; credit spreads and iron condors have negative means and positive medians (+$85, +$135); cumulative loss > $125 m of which > $90 m is transaction cost; the regression intercept of −0.141 % per trade becomes insignificant once a debit dummy (−0.29 %) and a complexity dummy are included. Fu et al. show customers supply liquidity with non-marketable limit orders in a quarter of all 0DTE trades and that a midpoint-rest-then-cross tactic costs $0.021–0.028 per contract against $0.05 for a marketable order in sub-$3 options: "the retail public may be losing money on these trades but they are not losing it to transactions costs."

### 3.6 Naranjo, Nimalendran, Wu — "Betting on Elusive Returns: Retail Trading in Complex Options" [WP, University of Florida; presented at the 2nd Structured Retail Products and Derivatives Conference 2024; numbers from the UF news summary and the conference abstract, SEC-SUM]

Retail multi-leg trade-level data "from a major options exchange." Complex trades were ≈ 20 % of retail option trades in July 2022; after commissions on multi-leg orders went to zero, retail-favoured complex volume rose > 75.4 % relative to other names; "Retail traders lose money over one-, two- and three-day periods, averaging a loss of **16.4%** over three-day periods," with losses increasing in the number of legs; 28 % of complex trades are around earnings and losses there are "three times larger." Registry cross-reference: S10-rerun (defined-risk verticals pay two spreads and are negative in every year) and S12b (short verticals −2.56/contract) are the local versions of "losses increase with complexity."

### 3.7 Ernst, Spatt — "Payment for Order Flow and Option Internalization" [WP NBER w29883 (2022) → PR *RFS* 2026, hhaf108]

From observed price-improvement-mechanism (PIM) option trades the authors draw, each day, 1,000 portfolios of 50 trades held to maturity (> 400,000 samples): "Over 75% of option trades lose money; over half of the option portfolios lose more than 90% or more of their value. Portfolios have a very positive skew, with 2.3% of option portfolios having a return exceeding 500%." The same construction from retail equity trades "delivers close to the market return"; option portfolios have ten times the standard deviation. This is the cleanest published statement of the *fraction of retail option trades that lose* when held to expiry.

### 3.8 Han, Lee, Liu — Taiwan stock index options 2002–2005 [WP, SSRN, Jan 2009; numbers from the CXO Advisory summary, SEC-SUM; the SSRN text could not be retrieved]

Complete transaction, order and quote records; 238,303 individual investors, 1,076 domestic institutions, 50 foreign institutions, 29 market makers. Individuals: average net return per trade **−3.4 %**; **about 27 %** of individual option traders achieve positive overall net returns; average trade duration under five days; individuals are on average successful only with short puts. Foreign institutions +14.3 % per trade, yet only 46 % of them are net positive overall. Long puts earn significantly less than short puts for every group; long calls less than short calls for every group except market makers. Context from the same market: Barber, Lee, Liu, Odean [PR, *RFS* 2009] find Taiwanese individual investors' aggregate equity trading losses equal 3.8 percentage points of return a year, 2.2 % of GDP.

### 3.9 SEBI (India) — equity futures and options, FY22–FY24 [EX; press release PR 37/2024 of 23 Sep 2024 confirmed on sebi.gov.in; table values from a forum reproduction of the study, SEC-SUM]

"93% of over 1 crore individual F&O traders incurred average losses of around Rs 2 lakh per trader (inclusive of transaction costs)" over FY22–FY24; aggregate losses > ₹1.8 lakh crore; the top 3.5 % of loss-makers (≈ 4 lakh traders) lost ≈ ₹28 lakh each; 7.2 % of traders were profitable and **1 %** earned more than ₹1 lakh after costs; the January 2023 study had found 89 % losing in FY22. FY24: individuals lost ≈ ₹55,000 crore in options and made ≈ ₹13,400 crore in futures; gross of costs, proprietary traders +₹33,000 crore and FPIs +₹28,000 crore, 96–97 % of it from algorithmic trading; individuals spent ≈ ₹26,000 per person on transaction costs in FY24 (₹50,000 crore over three years, 51 % brokerage, 20 % exchange fees). Persistence: 76.3 % of loss-makers kept trading after two loss years; of 24.4 lakh traders who lost in both FY22 and FY23, 18.6 lakh traded in FY24 and 8.3 % of those turned a profit. Demographics: 43 % under 30 (FY24), 76 % declaring income under ₹5 lakh, 85 % of the "very high income" group also lost. This is the largest regulator census of individual option-trader outcomes that exists; the market (index weeklies on Nifty/Bank Nifty, lot-sized, exchange-traded, no PFOF) is structurally different from the U.S., which makes the 93 % figure a statement about the product class rather than about U.S. market structure.

### 3.10 Synthesis: fraction of trades and of traders that lose

- Trades: > 75 % of retail PIM option trades lose when held to expiry (Ernst–Spatt); > 25 % of retail SPX 0DTE single-leg trades expire at −100 % and the median single-leg trade is −0.7 to −0.8 % (Beckmeyer); registry S01/S02/S09 win rates 31–38 % for the owner's long-0DTE rule, 58 % for short ATM straddles that still lose (S15), 71–73 % for the median short-premium grid configuration with a negative mean (H16). Win rate is set by structure and strike distance, not by edge (H16).
- Traders: 27 % of individual index-option traders net positive over four years (Taiwan); 7.2 % over three years (India); the Dutch panel shows persistent losers and a small persistent-winner group with negative *net* alphas even in the top deciles (Bauer: "Although gross alphas for investors in the top deciles are close to zero, the net alphas are negative"); no U.S. census exists — Bogousslavsky–Muravyev's journal users are a self-selected active population with −0.9 % per trade.
- The U.S. aggregate is negative in every month-type and weekday (Bryzgalova Table IA.XXXVII: losses "not concentrated in any particular month," lower when the holding period spans a weekend; Beckmeyer: net negative every weekday).

## 4. Why they lose: the decomposition

**(a) Spread.** The single largest term. Bryzgalova: indirect costs $6.4 bn against $2.1 bn of net losses — gross P&L is positive. Beckmeyer: ≈ 60 % of daily retail 0DTE losses are transaction costs; gross cumulative losses flat since Nov 2022 while net losses keep growing. de Silva: 9 of the 10–14 points lost on high-EAV earnings trades is the half-spread. Effective spreads retail actually pays: 6.6–6.7 % (ETF/equity marketable, 2019–21), 5–6 % (SPX 0DTE retail, 2021–23), 11.6 % on sub-$250 trades, 29 % slightly OTM; measured C1 half-spreads on 2025–26 chains: SPY/QQQ 0.5 %, IWM 0.7–1.0 %, SPXW 0.9 %, single names 3.5–4.1 %, RUTW 4.5 %, NDXP 7.5 %. Bogousslavsky–Muravyev's −0.9 % per trade against 5–10 % quoted spreads is the limit-order user's number; Fu et al. price the midpoint-rest tactic at roughly half the marketable cost.

**(b) Theta and the variance premium.** Coval–Shumway [PR]: ATM zero-beta index straddles lose ≈ 3 % per week. Muravyev–Ni [PR]: delta-hedged S&P 500 option returns −0.7 %/day, all of it overnight (−1 %/day close-to-open, +0.3 % open-to-close), for all maturities and moneyness and for equity options. Beckmeyer's debit dummy (−0.29 % per 0DTE trade) is the same premium measured on the retail side; Bryzgalova's sell portfolio is positive gross at every horizon. Registry: S15 and H9/H11/H13 show the 0DTE ATM premium on SPY/QQQ/SPXW is smaller than the marketable round trip at 10:00 and roughly equal to it at 13:00.

**(c) Timing and selection.** Bauer: option traders' underperformance is "bad market timing that results from overreaction to past stock market movements" — bearish for two years into a recovery. Barber, Huang, Odean, Schwarz [PR, *JF* 2022]: the top stocks Robinhood users buy each day earn −4.7 % abnormal over the next 20 days, −19.6 % after extreme herding (working-paper abstract); Robinhood outages disproportionately cut trading in high-attention stocks. Eaton, Green, Roseman, Wu [PR, *JFE* 146(2) 2022, 502–528]: Robinhood outages are followed by lower order imbalances, better liquidity and lower volatility in high-retail-interest stocks, the opposite after traditional-broker outages — "herding by inexperienced investors can create inventory risks that harm liquidity." de Silva: retail buys the events where implied variance has already risen 40 % more, then holds through the decay. Lakonishok et al. and Mahani–Poteshman: calls on growth stocks at the top. Registry: S04/S06 — every Compass trigger fires after +10 to +38 bp of prior movement and carries 0 ± 1 bp forward; S01's puts lose twice what calls lose; S09 the same on SPXW. The counterpoint is Welch [PR, *JF* 77(3) 2022]: the aggregate Robinhood equity consensus portfolio mid-2018 to mid-2020 had positive alpha and good timing — the equity crowd's *holdings* were fine; the *trades* at the attention margin (Barber et al.) and the *option* trades (everything above) were not.

**(d) Structure.** Buyers lose, sellers gain, and complexity costs: Bryzgalova (buy −8.7 to −12.7 $m/day net, sell +3.8 to +7.7), Beckmeyer (debit −$364 k/day, credit +$122 k/day; spreads and condors have positive medians and negative means), Bogousslavsky–Muravyev (purchases −3.95 %, naked sales +20 %), Han–Lee–Liu (individuals positive only in short puts), Naranjo et al. (−16.4 % on multi-leg). Registry mirrors: S10 single-contract fade +$0.52 (breakeven), S10-rerun verticals −$5.65, S12b short verticals −$2.56.

**(e) The arithmetic in underlying terms (registry S04).** A $2.80 round trip on a ~$1, 0.45-delta SPY contract is 2–3 bp of spot; with theta over a 60-minute hold the entry must carry a day-clustered lower bound of at least +3 bp over 30–60 minutes against the matched random control. No trigger in the catalogue reached +0.1. On a weekly contract the owner's rule is zero rather than negative (S03): remove the theta and what is left is the absence of a forecast.

## 5. Execution: PFOF and price improvement in options

**Size and concentration of PFOF.** Bryzgalova Fig. 1–2: PFOF from options exceeds PFOF from stocks by about 100 % in every month; 2021 options PFOF **$2.4 bn vs $1.3 bn** for equities; the top three wholesalers (Citadel, Susquehanna, Wolverine) reached nearly 90 % of options PFOF by Q2 2021, the top five > 95 %. Ernst–Spatt from Rule 606: "typical PFOF paid to a broker for routing a 100 share options trade at 40 cents, while the typical payment for routing a 100 share equity trade is around 20 cents"; a $1,000 investment in a $5 option generates 80 ¢ of PFOF against 8 ¢ for $1,000 in a $25 stock — ten times — so "brokers have … a particular incentive to encourage excess trading of options." Robinhood's 10-K for 2024 [EX]: options transaction revenue **$760 m** (+50 % from $505 m; 26 % of net revenues), driven by a 29 % rise in users placing option trades, a 43 % rise in contracts traded and "higher option rebate rates"; Q4 2024 options revenue $222 m on 477 m contracts = **$0.47 per contract**; Q3 2024 443.4 m contracts, Q2 389.7 m. Robinhood's Rule 606 report for Q2 2025 [EX], April options table (figures are 80 % of what the clearing affiliate receives): net payment per contract, market / marketable limit / non-marketable limit / other: Citadel 30.7 / 37.9 / 45.3 / 43.0 ¢; Dash-IMC 46.7 / 41.2 / 54.1 / 42.8; Wolverine 27.4 / 28.9 / 36.7 / 28.5; Morgan Stanley 34.8 / 40.4 / 50.7 / 49.6; Global Execution Brokers (Susquehanna) 48.8 / 39.4 / 52.2 / 44.6. Order mix at Robinhood: market 1.27 %, marketable limit 28.38 %, **non-marketable limit 56.37 %**, other 13.98 %. Trade press (GlobalTrading, S3 data) [PRAC]: market makers paid ≈ 56 ¢/contract for Robinhood flow in Q2 2025 against an industry average of 44.2 ¢. CCMR [PRAC, June 2025]: across six large brokers in 2024, market orders were 7.0–7.5 % of options orders, marketable limits 15 %, non-marketable limits 48–49 %, other 29 %; average PFOF 26.73 ¢ on market orders and 32.99 ¢ on non-marketable limits.

**Price improvement mechanisms.** Ernst–Spatt: all option trades are on-exchange; PIM auctions are ≈ 15 % of all trades and generate $200–600 m per month of improvement against the prevailing spread, $12.4 bn over their sample, on $770 bn of PIM trades in 26 months; but exchange rules (DMM first-five-contracts priority, price-match rights, reduced take fees) blunt competition, and on a $2 option "having a PFOF specialist leads to a price that is 100 basis points (or 2 cents) worse per share, while the broker collects 0.4 cents per share" — a 1 % worse price on $770 bn is > $7 bn, far more than the $2–3 bn of options PFOF in the period. Hendershott, Khan, Riordan [PR, *RFS* 39(3) 2026, 783 ff.; OPRA Aug–Dec 2020]: auctions are > 20 % of options volume (23 % of single-leg trading) and "save investors over $47 million daily"; wholesalers run more auctions when their recent improvement has been low and earn their PFOF in continuous (non-auction) internalized trades; "less than 10% [of auctions] having more than one bidder at the winning price," so 90 % show no competition at non-minimal improvement; an earlier draft measured auction improvement at 50 % of the quoted half-spread in S&P 500 names. Anand–Muravyev [WP]: auction trades are likelier when the auction exchange is off the NBBO and then less likely to be improved; restricting auctions narrowed quoted spreads with a muted effect on effective spreads.

**What a retail marketable order actually gets, measured.** Huang, Jorion, Schwarz, "Some Anonymous Options Trades Are More Equal than Others" [WP, SSRN 4951825; numbers from the authors' SEC comment letter of 16 April 2026]: ≈ 7,000 market and marketable-limit orders of varying size in two dozen symbols at six brokers, March–June 2024, placed with the authors' own money (Table 9): price improvement as % of the NBBO spread, overall / in auctions / in auto-execution, and PFOF in ¢ per contract — Vanguard 51.5 / 64.0 / 11.5, 0 ¢; Fidelity 40.5 / 51.0 / 9.1, 10.1 ¢; Schwab 33.2 / 44.4 / 7.9, 45.7 ¢; TD Ameritrade 19.0 / 35.3 / 5.0, 39.3 ¢; E*Trade 18.8 / 33.3 / 6.6, 38.9 ¢; **Robinhood 7.2 / 21.1 / 2.5, 65.8 ¢**; share of trades sent to auction 75.5 % (Vanguard) down to 25.1 % (Robinhood); correlation of improvement with PFOF −0.91. "Trade execution varied across our six brokers by almost the entire quoted spread (89%)." Robinhood's rebuttal (SSRN 5399225) reports its own improvement at 9.3 % for the symbols traded and 12.5 % for all flow; the authors say the rank order is unchanged and that an extended sample (> 20,000 trades, nine brokers) agrees. The CCMR critique is that market orders are only ≈ 7 % of retail option orders, so the result describes the marketable minority — which is exactly the order type the Compass fill model assumes. Since 2019 several brokers charge no commission and no per-contract fee, "making price execution the only determinant of trading costs."

**Implication for the registry's cost model.** C1 charges the full measured half-spread each way (ask in, bid out) plus $0.70 per contract per side: that is the Robinhood-marketable case (≈ 7 % improvement), which is conservative by roughly a third to a half of the spread for a Fidelity/Vanguard-routed marketable order (40–50 % improvement) and by about half for a midpoint-rest limit order (Fu et al.). No closed result changes sign under the generous case: S01's gross mean is −$2.79 before any cost; S10's single fade (+$0.52 on a $2.80 round trip) would move to roughly +$1.9 with an interval still spanning zero; H9 (+$8.60 against $12.93) and H11 (+$72 against $69) would gain $5–30 and remain inside their intervals; H15 was priced at $1 per contract per side with NBBO fills and is unaffected. Sign-flips are not available from execution quality; magnitude corrections are, and they belong in the engine as a stated scenario (see R1 below).

## 6. Robinhood and the 2020–21 cohort (regulator record)

SEC staff report on GME, 18 Oct 2021 [EX]: individual customer accounts were 91 % of non-market-maker GME option volume in mid-January 2021 and 56 % by late January; three brokers (Robinhood, TD Ameritrade, E*Trade) were > 66 % of individual accounts trading GME options; Robinhood and TD Ameritrade alone had over half the volume; on 27 January 2021 over 2 m GME contracts (> $8 bn notional) traded against a 2020 median of 16,000 contracts/day; the increase "concentrated heavily in call options, a large percentage of which were short-dated"; 50-delta implied volatility reached nine times the 2020 range; staff found no evidence of a gamma squeeze. Bryzgalova: January 2021 was retail's worst month (−$672 m), and retail lost on GME and AMC while the market as a whole gained on them. FINRA 22-08 [EX] records enforcement cases (an unsuitable options strategy costing two retail customers > $1.6 m; false spread-risk statements costing > $5 m) and the doubling of volume 2019→2021, and reminds members of the Rule 2360(b)(16) account-approval duty; the SEC report notes brokers "often" approve lower option levels by questionnaire.

## 7. Alert services, newsletters, social media and copy trading

**Investment newsletters (the only long-run audited record).** Graham and Harvey [PR, *JFE* 42(3) 1996, 397–421; NBER w4890]: 237 newsletters' equity/cash recommendations, 1980–1992 (15,172 recommendations): no evidence that letters shift equity weight ahead of market moves; the letters carry no information about future market returns; only their disagreement predicts realized and implied volatility. Jaffe and Mahoney [PR, *JFE* 1999; Hulbert Financial Digest 1980–96]: recommended securities do not outperform appropriate benchmarks; raw-return persistence exists but vanishes on abnormal returns; poor performers exit (survivorship). Metrick [PR, *JF* 1999; NBER w6648]: 153 newsletters over 17 years, "no significant evidence of superior stock-picking ability"; strong individual records occur no more often than chance; buying past winners earns no abnormal return.

**Finfluencers.** Kakhbod, Kazempour, Livdan, Schürhoff [WP, Swiss Finance Institute 23-30, 2023–24; tweet-level data]: 28 % of finfluencers are skilled (+2.6 % monthly abnormal return on their advice), 16 % unskilled (zero), **56 % "antiskilled" (−2.3 % per month)**; antiskilled finfluencers have more followers and more influence on retail trading; a contrarian strategy against the antiskilled earns ≈ 1.2 % per month out of sample.

**Reddit WSB.** Bradley, Hanousek, Jame, Xiao [PR, *Review of Financial Studies* 2024; working paper 2021]: due-diligence posts 2018–2020 earn +1.1 % over two days, +2 % over a month, ≈ 5 % over a quarter; "This predictability is eliminated post-GME," when price-pressure reports rose 165 % and attention-stock reports 75 %.

**Copy trading.** Apesteguia, Oechssler, Weidenholzer [PR, *Management Science* 66(12) 2020, 5608–5622]: a laboratory experiment — seeing others' success raises risk-taking and the option to copy raises it further; no field evidence on follower returns was found in this sweep beyond platform-sponsored material [VEND] and student theses.

**Pump-and-dump through chat channels.** Hamrick et al. [WP, CEPR DP13404 / WEIS 2019]: 3,767 pump signals on Telegram and 1,051 on Discord in six months of 2018, > 300 coins, "widespread and often quite profitable" for organisers. Dhawan and Putniņš [PR, *Review of Finance* 27(3) 2023, 935–975]: 355 pumps on Binance and Yobit, average price distortion 65 %, "a negative-sum game where most participants, except manipulators, incur losses," explained by overconfidence and gambling preference. SEC/DOJ, 14 Dec 2022 [EX, via AP]: eight Twitter/Discord promoters with > 1.5 m followers charged over a > $100 m scheme, selling into the demand they created from early 2020 to April 2022. FTC v. RagingBull.com [EX]: a $2.425 m order (March 2022) over claims that its "gurus" could double or triple accounts in a week; the FTC found the company "didn't track customers' trading results, so the defendants had no way to support their claims"; one guru's tips "cost consumers more than $40 million" (FTC, Sept 2023); RagingBull sold stock and options alert subscriptions [PRAC description]. 9,862 refund payments were sent in the first round.

**Options alert services specifically.** No peer-reviewed or working-paper study of options alert or signal services (Discord, Telegram, SMS) was found; the one academic result on social-media "signals" is crypto-only (Springer 2025, signal-level event study, no follower costs). Everything else is vendor material: e.g., a Discord SPX-0DTE alert room advertising "69.4 % win rate across 1,174 trades" [VEND, alphapodtrading.com], aggregator lists of "best" alert rooms [VEND, tradealgo.com, whop.com], and a guide citing "a 2025 FINRA study" and "a 2024 academic study" with no title, author or link [VEND; not locatable]. The registry's own position on this class is H12/RULE-separation (an operator's reported P&L is not evidence about a hypothesis) and G1 (reported +$39,297 → executable +$12,039 with 27 % of same-minute repeats priced below the minute's lowest bid). The newsletter literature is the right prior: audited, long-run, zero.

**"Unusual options activity" feeds.** Vendor claims that unusual-volume or sweep flow predicts returns are marketing [VEND]; the owner's own tests of Unusual Whales flow features are closed (H2: 0 of 62 cells survive correction, forward ICs ≤ 0.02; H3: burst contracts −6 to −8 % vs −4 to −6 % random). Bryzgalova's finding that *retail* call imbalances predict next-day stock returns is attributed by the authors to dealer hedging pressure, not information, and is one day long.

## 8. Institutional-only versus retail-accessible

Institutional-only (cannot be replicated with marketable or resting orders at the NBBO, $0.70 per contract, no rebates): the wholesaler's realized spread on internalized retail flow (Hendershott et al.: PFOF is earned in continuous trades where DMM status gives priority; Ernst–Spatt: first-five-contracts allocation, price-match rights); auction-response and quote-competition revenue; the market maker's side of de Silva's earnings-vol overpricing (requires warehousing jump risk across hundreds of names with inventory netting); Bryzgalova's one-day return predictability from aggregate retail imbalances (requires OPRA-level flagged flow); anything using Cboe participant-level positions or Nasdaq clientele volumes.

Retail-accessible, with the size the literature puts on it: (i) order type and broker — the difference between 7 % and 52 % of the NBBO spread on a marketable order, and about half the marketable cost by resting at the midpoint (Huang–Jorion–Schwarz; Fu et al.) — a cost reduction, not an expectation; (ii) writing the sub-week options retail buys — positive net in aggregate but not significant beyond intraday (Bryzgalova), +$122 k/day against −$364 k/day for buyers on SPX 0DTE (Beckmeyer), +20 % on naked sales in the journal data (Bogousslavsky–Muravyev) — and the registry's measured version on index 0DTE is breakeven to negative at the marketable round trip (S10, S15, H9, H11, H13), positive only for the unpromotable carry lane H15; (iii) being on the other side of earnings-vol overpricing in single names — de Silva's 11 % + 9 % straddle underperformance is the only documented retail-side mispricing with a size larger than the single-name round trip (2 × 3.5–4.1 % of premium), and it is untested here (R2, R3 below); (iv) avoiding the losing cells — index 0DTE puts (Beckmeyer: ≈ 60 % of losses; S01 puts −5.89 vs calls −2.26), slightly OTM and sub-$250 contracts (29 % and 23.5 % quoted spreads), multi-leg structures (−16.4 %/3 days), attention names after herding (−4.7 %/20 days), and holding through post-event decay (−9 %/2 weeks).

## 9. Hypotheses that survive, and what is closed

**R1 — Calibrate the fill model with measured small-print execution (cost input, not a lane).** Claim: the registry prices every fill at the NBBO ask/bid; identified retail marketable fills on ETF and index options print inside the quote by ≈ 50 % of the half-spread on average (Bryzgalova effective/quoted 6.6/12.6; Beckmeyer 6.0/11.8 for SPX calls; Hendershott 50 % of the half-spread in auctions) but by only 7 % at the broker with the worst execution and 52 % at the best (Huang–Jorion–Schwarz). Test with our data: ThetaData option trade-quote prints (every print with the NBBO at the print, from 2012-06-04) on SPY/QQQ/IWM/SPXW — measure, for prints of ≤ 10 contracts, the distribution of (trade price − mid)/half-spread by product, year, moneyness and time of day, separating auction-coded prints where the condition codes allow; produce an effective-to-quoted ratio per cell and re-state C1 as three scenarios (NBBO, measured-small-print, midpoint-rest à la Fu). Expected effect: a 30–50 % reduction in the spread term of the round trip; no sign change in any closed study (§5), so it is recorded as a stated scenario in the engine rather than a re-opening. Retail-accessible: yes, by broker choice and order type. Window: 2012 → 2026 (prints), 2025–26 for the C1 comparison.

**R2 — Sell the pre-earnings volatility retail overpays for (mirror of de Silva).** Claim: ATM straddles on announcements with high expected abnormal volatility underperform low-EAV announcements by 11 % through the event (t = 19.55), and implied variance is 40 % more elevated where retail purchases are heaviest; a seller at the pre-event close collects what the buyer overpays. Not closed: S08 and S11 are *post*-event directional; S13/A5.2 is the pre-event *long* side sold at the pre-event close (it would be this trade's counterparty); S15 and H9–H13 are index 0DTE, not single-name events. Test with our data: S07 universe × the Unusual Whales earnings calendar (report dates and pre/post-market time, 1996 →); per event pull the nearest expiration chain (option EOD or 1-minute NBBO, 2012 →), sell the ATM straddle (and a 0.25-delta strangle) at the pre-event close at the bid, buy back at the post-event close at the ask, $0.70 per side per leg; rank events by implied move ÷ trailing realized announcement move (the EAV proxy) and by pre-event IV rise; day-clustered CIs, worst-event stress (the ±10 %/±15 % shocks of GATE-v2 applied to single names mean full-loss cells), Reg-T margin per lot. Expected size after costs: gross ≈ 5–10 % of straddle premium on the high-EAV tercile (de Silva's 11 % relative differential less the unconditional earnings premium), minus a round trip of ≈ 7–8 % of premium at measured single-name half-spreads — a thin positive at best, concentrated in the tail of jump losses; the retail-accessible version is the defined-risk iron condor, which pays four spreads and, per S10-rerun, loses the difference. Window: 2024 → 2026 now (daily lake), 2012 → as the daily years land.

**R3 — Sell the post-event decay retail holds through.** Claim: after high-EAV announcements straddles underperform a further 9 % over two weeks (t = 8.26) because retail keeps its long positions and dealers keep charging for inventory. Not closed (S11 "sell the beat" is directional and uses the surprise sign). Test: same events as R2; sell the ATM straddle at the first post-event close, buy back 10 sessions later or on a 2× credit stop; compare high- vs low-EAV terciles and against the matched non-event 10-session short straddle on the same names (the unconditional single-name VRP). Expected size after costs: de Silva's 9 % is relative to low-EAV events, not absolute; with 7–8 % of premium in round trip and ten sessions of gap risk the absolute cell is likely within noise — run because its cost is one pass over the R2 chains. Window as R2.

**Closed or inapplicable (with the registry id or the reason):**
- Long options on technical entries at any expiration: closed (S01, S02, S03, S09; Beckmeyer debit rows; Bogousslavsky–Muravyev purchases −3.95 %, 0DTE −4.7 %).
- Exit-rule or parameter rescue of a long-option entry: closed (H7, S02).
- Index 0DTE short straddles, unconditional or VRP-timed, at the marketable round trip: closed (H9, H11, H13, S15); fades of the owner's signal: breakeven/negative (S10, S10-rerun); first-half-hour expressions (S12, S12b).
- "Be the writer of the weeklies retail buys" as a generic edge: the buyer's loss is the spread and the writer pays the same spread; Bryzgalova's sell portfolio is net positive but insignificant beyond intraday; the index version is the closed set above. Only the event-conditioned single-name version (R2/R3) is open.
- Fading Robinhood herding / attention names (Barber et al. −4.7 %/20 days): not testable — no retail holdings or app-level data; Robintrack ended August 2020; UW flow proxies are closed (H2, H3).
- Contrarian to finfluencers or WSB (Kakhbod 1.2 %/month; Bradley post-GME zero): not testable — no social data and no API credential permitted; the published effects are equity-level and have decayed.
- Flow-following from "unusual activity" feeds: closed (H2, H3); vendor claims are [VEND].
- Multi-leg complexity as a source of edge: closed in the opposite direction (S10-rerun, S12b; Naranjo et al. −16.4 %).
- Lottery/OTM exclusion and weekend-theta cells: already carried by the companion note (`academic-anomalies.md` L4, L6) — not duplicated here.
- Midpoint limit-order route and execution timing (Fu et al.; Muravyev–Pearson): carried by `0dte-microstructure.md` and `academic-anomalies.md`; R1 above measures the empirical print distribution they imply.
- Broker/route selection: operational, not a backtest; GATE-v2's forward paper-trading gate logs live fills, which is where the 7–52 % improvement range is measured for this account.
- Options alert services: no study exists; audited newsletter record is zero; regulator cases show vendors did not track results. Nothing to test.
