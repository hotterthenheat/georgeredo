# L5 — option-implied stock-return predictors (smirk, call−put IV spread, ΔCVOL): evidence-quality refutation (2026-10-14)

Lens: evidence quality (is the cited effect real, replicated, not a data-mining artefact; checked against the primary text where it could be retrieved). Hypothesis under test: registry-sweep id **L5** as stated in `academic-anomalies.md` §4/§11 — "Option prices predict stock returns: steepest-smirk quintile underperforms flattest by 16 bp/week raw, 21 bp/week FF3 alpha (10.9 %/yr), 1996–2005 (Xing–Zhang–Zhao); call-minus-put IV spread long-short 50 bp/week, declining over the sample (Cremers–Weinbaum); past-month change in call IV decile spread 1.09 %/month raw, 1.38 % controlling for put IV, decaying to 0.34 % by month four, 1996–2011 (An–Ang–Bali–Cakici). Goncalves-Pinto et al. 2020 attribute the IV-gap predictability mainly to one-day stock price pressure/reversal (85 bp next-day alpha) rather than option-market information. Stock-side only; a long-option expression fails the premium/theta/spread arithmetic." Suggested test: S07-style deciles of smirk, IV spread and Δcall-IV at the session close, entry next open, 5/10/21-session absolute and universe-adjusted returns against the A2 hurdle, decay by year. Proposition to refute: *a retail-accessible option edge that is plausibly positive after costs and testable with our data over a window long enough for our gates.*

## 0. Verdict

**Refuted as a retail-accessible option edge — and the hypothesis concedes this in its own last sentence ("Stock-side only; a long-option expression fails the arithmetic"). As a stock-side screen it is real in the 1996–2011 OptionMetrics samples but (i) already decayed inside the samples, (ii) now attributed by two later peer-reviewed papers to borrow fees and one-day stock price pressure rather than to option-market information, (iii) not measurable with the precision the sorts need from single-name closing quotes on our own cache, and (iv) unresolvable on the 2024–2026 window at any plausible size. Expected size after costs: negative for every option expression; roughly 0–8 bp per week on the stock long leg, inside a stock round trip and inside measurement noise.** Six findings:

1. **No option expression exists, and the obvious one is wrong-signed before costs.** Compass's scope is long options (`08_port_spec_3abida.md` §5; `09_final_specification.md`: short-premium and stock-side leads are "outside the long-option scope until the owner decides"). The sweep note's arithmetic for the long-call expression stands: a one-week ATM single-name call at 35 % IV is ≈ 2.0 % of spot, pays 14–16 bp of spot in C1 half-spreads per round trip plus theta, against a 0.5-delta capture of ≈ 12 bp from a 25 bp/week stock move; a 30-DTE call held a week pays 28–32 bp of spread and ≈ 51 bp of theta. Doran, Fodor & Jiang (RAPS 2013 [PR]) add the sign: the call−put IV spread that predicts *stock* returns positively "is negatively related to future out-of-the-money call option returns" — buying calls on high-IV-spread names is the trade the paper's customer investors lose on. Nothing in the registry (S01–S16b) is a promoted option lane that a stock-side direction filter could improve.
2. **The published sizes are in-sample and already stale within their own samples.** Cremers–Weinbaum's 50 bp/week is 1996–2005 and, per An et al.'s description of it, "becoming insignificant over the second half of their sample, 2001–2005" (AABC NBER 19590 §V.B); the published abstract itself says "the degree of predictability decreases over the sample period". Xing–Zhang–Zhao's headline sort is t = 2.19 (16 bp/week, 520 weeks); Chen–Zimmermann's open-source replication grades the predictor "2_likely" with reproduction quality "2_fair" and notes "Screens in appendix are important" (`skew1`, `SignalDoc.csv`). Only An–Ang–Bali–Cakici's ΔCVOL (1.09 %/month, t 3.45; dependent-sort 1.38 %, t 5.85) is strong and reported robust in 1996–2003 / 2004–2011 and in 2006–2011; it is a *monthly* equal-weighted decile sort on the OptionMetrics volatility surface, and the paper itself says "there may be some liquidity frictions involved in implementing a tradable strategy".
3. **Two later peer-reviewed papers reassign the mechanism to things a retail stock position cannot harvest.** Muravyev, Pearson & Pollet, "Why does options market information predict stock returns?", *JFE* 172 (2025) 104153 [PR]: option prices reflect stock borrow fees, which are known to predict returns; "Adjusting returns for borrow fees reduces abnormal return predictability from options signals by about two-thirds. Excluding high-fee stocks reduces the predictability of unadjusted returns by a similar amount." Goncalves-Pinto et al., *Management Science* 66(9) 2020 [PR]: the cross-market gap's predictability "is strongest when stock price pressure is present and does not depend on options trading"; "stock price pressure is the primary driver". Lin & Lu, *JBF* 52 (2015) [PR]: the IV predictability "more than doubles" around analyst-related events. The residual information channel is therefore at most one-third of a 16–50 bp/week long-short, concentrated in the short (hard-to-borrow, overpriced) leg and around events; the long leg of a weekly sort — the only leg a retail account would hold in stock — is where the least of it lives (XZZ Table 3: the low-smirk quintile's FF3 alpha is 10 bp/week against −11 bp for the high-smirk quintile).
4. **The signal cannot be read from single-name closing quotes with the precision the sort needs [LOCAL].** On the S11 option-EOD cache (1,167 name-days, 476 roots of the S07 universe, 30–50 DTE, 2023-12 → 2026-10, closing NBBO, spot from the stock-EOD lake): the OTM put nearest K/S = 0.95 has a median closing half-spread of 20.7 % of mid (p75 60 %; 202 of 1,167 are bid $0.00) and the ATM call 10.0 % (p75 23 %). Converting bid and ask to implied volatilities (BS bisection, r = 0.04), the quote-implied half-width of the XZZ smirk is **4.6 vol points at the median** (p25 2.2, p75 8.6) on the 931 name-days where both legs are two-sided, and the same 4.5 vol points for the ATM call−put spread. XZZ's quintile mean smirks are −0.34 / 2.87 / 4.79 / 7.55 / 17.14 vol points: adjacent quintiles are 2–3 points apart, so the closing quote cannot place a median name in its quintile; 42.5 % of name-days have a smirk interval that straddles XZZ's own median smirk (4.76 %). Only 9 of 476 roots (IWM, NVDA, SMH, GOOGL, GLD, IBM, RACE, AMZN among them) have a median half-width ≤ 1 vol point. Intraday 15:45 single-name quotes are ≈ 3× tighter than the close (C1 3.5–4.1 % vs the 10.8 % closing median, `execution-costs.md` §2.2), which still leaves ≈ 1.5 vol points on the median name, and measuring the whole universe at 15:45 means one whole-chain 1-minute NBBO request per name per session (3–6 s each): ≈ 1,050 login-hours for 2024–2026 alone (§3). The published papers avoid this problem by using OptionMetrics' interpolated volatility surface (AABC) or vendor IVs with liquidity screens (XZZ) on a large-cap-tilted sample; our chain-level replication would be noisier than theirs, not cleaner.
5. **The available window cannot resolve the effect even at full published size, and the A2 hurdle cannot be reached [LOCAL arithmetic].** XZZ's 16 bp/week at t = 2.19 over 520 weeks implies a weekly spread standard deviation of ≈ 166 bp. On the window available now (2024-01 → 2026-10, ≈ 140 weekly sorts) the standard error is ≈ 14 bp/week and the 95 % half-width ≈ 28 bp: a 16 bp mean has a lower bound of −12 bp; to clear the adjusted part of A2 (lower bound > 0) the mean would need to be ≥ 28 bp/week, 1.75× the published long-short and ≈ 3× the long-leg alpha. On the full 2012-06 → 2026-10 history (≈ 745 weeks, once the 2012–2022 stock-EOD years and the option EOD per name are in) the standard error is ≈ 6 bp: the full published 16 bp would be t ≈ 2.6, but the one-third that survives the borrow-fee adjustment (≈ 5 bp/week) is t ≈ 0.9. AABC's monthly sort behaves the same way (1.09 %/month, t 3.45 over 192 months → monthly sd ≈ 4.4 %; on 34 month-ends the half-width is ≈ 1.5 %; S14's 20–34 rebalance dates already produced intervals "hundreds of bp wide"). The ≥ 150-session and ≥ 7-month gates are met by the window; the statistical-validity gate is not reachable on it.
6. **The test is expensive and competes with the pulls the daily studies are waiting on.** The suggested screen needs option EOD per name per session: one `option_history_eod` request per root per expiration per month, ≈ 1,200 roots × 36 months ≈ 43 k requests for 2024–2026 and ≈ 206 k for 2012–2026; at the S11 pull's observed rate (1,195 event files between 20:34 and 23:08 UTC today, ≈ 470 per hour on the single login) that is ≈ 90 and ≈ 440 login-hours (4 and 18 days) during which the 2022 → 2012 stock-EOD backfill that S07, S08, S13 and S14 need stays paused (`07_coverage_report.md` §1). The payoff, at best, is a stock-side direction filter for daily families that have no promoted lane to filter.

What survives: nothing for the Compass scope. The smirk belongs in this family and not in L4's exclusion rule (L4 §3 already says so). If the owner later admits stock positions, ΔCVOL (An et al.) is the only one of the three with a sub-period record through 2011 and a t-statistic above 3, and the honest expectation for a long-only monthly decile in 2012–2026 is a fraction of 1.38 %/month, measured on a volatility surface we would have to build.

## 1. The claims as cited versus the primary texts

### 1.1 Xing, Zhang & Zhao, "What Does the Individual Option Volatility Smirk Tell Us About Future Equity Returns?", *JFQA* 45(3) 2010, 641–662 [PR]; full text read

https://www.pbcsf.tsinghua.edu.cn/__local/A/24/EA/815CC33800CAA0F7D894E9292A1_6CC8B38B_27B25.pdf?e=.pdf. Sample January 1996 – December 2005, OptionMetrics end-of-day quotes, vendor binomial-tree IVs; on average 840 firms per week; the sample "is tilted toward large firms" (average annualized stock volatility 47.14 % against 57 % for all stocks). SKEW = IV of the OTM put (strike/stock in (0.80, 0.95), the one closest to 0.95) minus IV of the ATM call (strike/stock in [0.95, 1.05], closest to 1), options with 10–60 days to expiration, positive volume and open interest, IV between 0.03 and 2.0, price > $0.125 (Appendix); averaged Tuesday close to Tuesday close; one day skipped; value-weighted quintile returns Wednesday close to Wednesday close. Mean SKEW 6.40 %, median 4.76 % (volume-weighted version 5.70 % / 5.05 %; correlation 80 %). **Table 3 Panel A** (168 stocks per quintile): weekly excess returns 24 / 15 / 16 / 11 / 8 bp from low to high SKEW; Low − High **16 bp per week (9.19 %/yr), t −2.19**; FF3 alphas 10 and −11 bp, long-short **21 bp (10.90 %/yr), t 2.93**; quintile mean SKEW −0.34 / 2.87 / 4.79 / 7.55 / 17.14 %; Panel B (volume-weighted SKEW, 68 firms per quintile) 19 bp, t 2.05 / 2.07. Fama–MacBeth (Table 2): coefficient −0.0061 (t −2.50), an interquartile move in SKEW (2.40 % → 8.43 %) worth −5.52 bp per week; with controls −0.0089 (t −3.86). Persistence (Table 4 Panel B, cumulative FF3 alphas, annualized): 10.90 % at one week, **6.52 % at four weeks ("almost 40 % smaller")**, 6–7 % from 8 to 28 weeks (t 2.7–4.3); SKEW autocorrelation 0.66 at one week falling to ≈ 0.2 by lag 8. Section IV: steep-smirk firms have the worst subsequent earnings surprises. **No transaction-cost treatment anywhere** (the word "cost" does not occur; "profit" once, about informed traders), **no sub-period results** in the text, and the sample ends in 2005.

Evidence-quality reading: a peer-reviewed sort with a marginal headline t-statistic (2.19; a 3.0 threshold is the post-Harvey–Liu–Zhu norm for a new predictor — Harvey, Liu & Zhu, *RFS* 29(1) 2016, not retrieved this turn), large-cap-tilted, value-weighted, pre-2006, and no out-of-sample period in the paper. Chen–Zimmermann reproduce it as "2_likely" with "2_fair" signal quality (§1.9).

### 1.2 Cremers & Weinbaum, "Deviations from Put-Call Parity and Stock Return Predictability", *JFQA* 45(2) 2010, 335–367 [PR]; abstract only

https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v45y2010i02p335-367_00.html ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S002210901000013X/type/journal_article (DOI 10.1017/S002210901000013X). Abstract: the implied-volatility difference between matched call and put pairs predicts returns; "stocks with relatively expensive calls outperform stocks with relatively expensive puts by 50 basis points per week"; both legs earn abnormal returns; not explained by short-sale constraints (rebate rates checked); stronger when option liquidity is high and stock liquidity low; deviations more likely when information risk is higher; "the degree of predictability decreases over the sample period", read by the authors as mispricing that gradually shrinks. Sample period not on either page; An et al. (§1.3, NBER text) state that Cremers and Weinbaum's predictability "declines during their sample period becoming insignificant over the second half of their sample, 2001–2005", which fixes the sample at 1996–2005 and the effect as a 1996–2000 phenomenon in the paper's own data. Not carried by Chen–Zimmermann; the same construct (ATM call IV minus ATM put IV) is carried as Bali–Hovakimian's `CPVolSpread` (§1.6). The CBS thesis cited in the sweep note as a European non-replication is [SEC] and was not re-read.

### 1.3 An, Ang, Bali & Cakici, "The Joint Cross Section of Stocks and Options", *Journal of Finance* 69(5) 2014, 2279–2338 [PR]; NBER WP 19590 (2013) read in full [WP version]

https://www.nber.org/papers/w19590.pdf. Sample January 1996 – December 2011; 1,261 stocks per month in 1996 rising to 2,312 in 2011; signals from the OptionMetrics Volatility Surface (30-day, 0.5-delta call and put IVs at month end; actual-option IVs in the internet appendix "are similar"); equal-weighted decile portfolios rebalanced monthly. **Table II:** ΔCVOL deciles raw 0.29 % (low) → 1.38 % (high), **spread 1.09 %/month, Newey–West t 3.45**; CAPM alpha 1.04 %, FF3 1.00 %, characteristic-adjusted 0.86 % (t 2.87). ΔPVOL spread −0.42 % (t −2.03); ΔPVOL − ΔCVOL spread −1.68 % (t −6.77; FF3 −1.65 %). **Table III** (dependent sorts): ΔCVOL controlling for ΔPVOL **1.38 % (t 5.85)**, FF3 1.36 % (t 5.22); ΔPVOL controlling for ΔCVOL −1.04 % (t −6.40). **Table V** (overlapping Jegadeesh–Titman holdings): ΔCVOL spread falls "by more than a half" from 1.38 % in month 1 to 0.63 % in month 2 and 0.34 % after four months; ΔPVOL −1.04 % → −0.47 % in month 2, gone after three months. Sub-periods (internet appendix, described in §V.B): 1996–2003 / 2004–2011 and 1996–2000 / 2001–2005 / 2006–2011, slopes "positive (negative) and highly significant for all subsample periods including 2001–2005"; the introduction says the predictability "is still seen in recent data" through the 2008–2009 crisis. Liquidity: effects are smaller but present in large and liquid stocks; "The reduction, but not elimination, of the anomalous returns in the larger and more liquid stocks indicates that there may be some liquidity frictions involved in implementing a tradable strategy"; the internet appendix drops the smallest, least liquid and lowest-priced stocks and keeps significance. No explicit transaction-cost accounting in the main text.

Reading: the strongest of the three (t > 5 in the dependent sort, sub-periods through 2011, decay profile stated). Its signal is a *monthly* change in a surface-interpolated ATM IV, equal-weighted across ≈ 230 stocks per decile; the one-month ΔCVOL has low persistence, so a decile portfolio turns over almost entirely each month — fine for stock round trips of 2–5 bp against 1.38 %, not a fact about options. It ends in 2011, before the Muravyev–Pearson–Pollet borrow-fee decomposition (§1.5).

### 1.4 Goncalves-Pinto, Grundy, Hameed, van der Heijden & Zhu, "Why Do Option Prices Predict Stock Returns? The Role of Price Pressure in the Stock Market", *Management Science* 66(9) 2020, 3903–3926 [PR]; abstract only

https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v66y2020i9p3903-3926.html ; Semantic Scholar record for DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2019.3398 (open-access pointer to SSRN 2695145, which returned 403). Abstract: the stock and options markets can disagree about a stock's value because of informed options trading, stock price pressure or both; the return predictability of the cross-market gap "is strongest when stock price pressure is present and does not depend on options trading"; option-implied prices anchor fundamental values, separating pressure-driven moves from news; "stock price pressure is the primary driver of option price-based return predictability". **The sweep note's "85 bp alpha on the day after formation" and the "DOTS" label could not be verified** from any page reachable this turn; the qualitative attribution is verified. The implication for L5 is unchanged: what the IV gap mostly forecasts is a one-day reversal of a stock-side price dislocation, which a weekly or 5–21-session holding at the next open captures only partially and which the S07 engine's next-open entry (not the same close) partly skips.

### 1.5 Muravyev, Pearson & Pollet, "Why does options market information predict stock returns?", *Journal of Financial Economics* 172 (2025) 104153 [PR]; abstract only

Semantic Scholar record for DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2025.104153 (the Elsevier redirect was not followed); citation confirmed on Pearson's faculty page https://giesbusiness.illinois.edu/profile/neil-pearson, which also lists the companion "Anomalies and Their Short-Sale Costs", *JF* 80(6) 2025. Abstract: several well-known studies find that options-implied volatilities and trading volume forecast stock returns, which is puzzling because the data are public; the predictability "is consistent with option prices reflecting stock borrow fees, which are known to predict returns"; the authors derive a formula linking the option-implied volatility spread to the borrow fee; "Adjusting returns for borrow fees reduces abnormal return predictability from options signals by about two-thirds. Excluding high-fee stocks reduces the predictability of unadjusted returns by a similar amount." Sample period and the list of signals are not in the abstract; the IV spread is named. This is the most recent peer-reviewed statement on the family and it is the one that matters for a retail stock position: the predictable part is mostly the short-leg return that a lender's fee already prices, and a retail long in the "cheap-put" leg does not receive it.

### 1.6 Bali & Hovakimian, "Volatility Spreads and Expected Stock Returns", *Management Science* 55(11) 2009, 1797–1812 [PR]; abstract only

https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v55y2009i11p1797-1812.html. ## 2. Evidence-quality reading across the sources

- **Real in-sample, with one data vendor and overlapping samples.** Three teams, one source (OptionMetrics closing quotes or its volatility surface), 1996–2005 / 1996–2005 / 1996–2011; Chen–Zimmermann reproduce the sorts. This is not a single-team data-mining artefact, but neither is it three independent samples: the 1996–2005 decade is common to all of them and is the decade in which Cremers–Weinbaum's own effect disappears by 2001.
- **Magnitude and significance are weaker than the sweep note's headline suggests.** XZZ t 2.19 (grade 2_likely); CW insignificant in 2001–2005; only AABC clears t 3 and shows 2006–2011 robustness. The long leg (what a retail stock account would hold) is the smaller half of each spread: XZZ 10 bp/week FF3 alpha vs −11 bp on the short leg; AABC's deciles 0.29 % → 1.38 % are raw returns in an equal-weighted small-stock-heavy sample.
- **The mechanism has been reassigned.** MPP 2025: two-thirds is borrow fees. Goncalves-Pinto 2020: price pressure, not options trading. Lin–Lu 2015: analyst events. None of these is harvestable by buying the "cheap-put" stocks for a week at the next open with marketable orders.
- **Measurement.** The papers use vendor IVs on liquidity-screened, large-cap-tilted samples or an interpolated surface; closing single-name quotes in 2024–2026 give a smirk half-width of 4.6 vol points on the median S07-universe name (§3), and Goyenko–Zhang's closing-spread bias (`academic-anomalies.md` 0.2) applies to the inputs. A chain-level replication at the close would be a noisier instrument than the originals.
- **No post-2011 peer-reviewed replication of the weekly smirk/IV-spread long leg was retrieved** (search budget exhausted; the one 2025 paper found reduces it by two-thirds). The honest statement is: the effect's existence after 2011 at the published horizon is unverified here, and the best available evidence says it is mostly a borrow-fee and price-pressure premium.

## 5. What would change the verdict

- A peer-reviewed or well-documented working-paper replication on 2012 → 2024 OptionMetrics or ORATS data of the *long* leg of the smirk or IV-spread sort net of borrow fees, with t > 3 at a weekly horizon — none was retrievable this turn; MPP 2025 points the other way.
- The owner admitting stock positions to the Compass scope: then ΔCVOL (An et al.) is the candidate, tested monthly on the full 2012 → 2026 lake once the stock-EOD years land, on a surface we would have to build from chains (the vendor IV floor is 2020), with the borrow-fee caveat stated up front and the expectation of ≈ 0.3–0.5 %/month on the long decile before stock costs.
- A Tier A 15:45 measurement showing a smirk half-width ≤ 1 vol point on ≥ 100 names (≈ 90 login-hours for 2024–2026). Even then the use is a stock-side direction filter, which needs a lane.
- The sweep's own cost-realism note for the long-option expression stands and is not what this note disputes; it is confirmed by Doran et al.'s sign.
