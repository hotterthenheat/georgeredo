# L6 — weekend / nontrading-period option decay (Jones–Shemesh): evidence-quality refutation (2026-10-14)

Lens: evidence quality (is the cited effect real, replicated, not a data-mining artefact; checked against the primary text where it could be retrieved). Hypothesis under test: sweep id **L6** as stated in `academic-anomalies.md` §6/§11 — "Delta-hedged option returns are sharply negative over weekends and holidays and about zero on weekdays: delta-hedged puts −0.82 % per nontrading period vs +0.03 % per trading day (t −11.93), calls −0.48 % vs +0.03 %, 59 of 60 delta/maturity portfolios, negative every year 1996–2014, present in SPX puts (−1.07 %, t −4.56) and in options with ≤ 5 % spreads (−0.63 %, t −9.24); mechanism is calendar-time pricing of variance (gamma-loaded). Muravyev–Ni: SPX delta-hedged −1 %/day overnight, +0.3 % intraday (2004–2013). Goyenko–Zhang: closing-quote inventory bias inflates these by up to 100 bp/day and the weekend effect shrinks or evaporates at 10 am quotes post-2015. Authors state it is not a stand-alone strategy at market orders; it is a timing rule for a long-option holder." Suggested test: delta-hedged return of the baseline-rule weekly contract and the ATM straddle, Friday 15:50 → Monday 09:35 vs Monday–Thursday 15:50 → next 09:35, at NBBO mid and bid/ask, with 10:00 marks, by year and product; adopt as an execution rule if the weekend-minus-weekday difference has a positive lower bound ≥ 0.3 % of premium. Proposition to refute: *a retail-accessible option edge that is plausibly positive after costs and testable with our data over a window long enough for our gates.*

## 0. Verdict

**Refuted as a retail-accessible option edge. The nontrading effect is real in its own 1996–2014 closing-quote sample and internally robust, but (i) the paper itself says it is not a stand-alone strategy at market orders and frames it only as "the benefit from delaying the purchase by one day"; (ii) the only replication with intraday marks cuts the weekend-minus-weekday gap by 50–80 % and the magnitudes at 10 am marks are contradicted by a later intraday study, so the executable size is unknown; (iii) the instrument the hypothesis would trade — a 1–7-DTE index-ETF weekly — is outside every published sample (Jones–Shemesh's shortest bucket is 11–53 trading days), and on our own 2017–2026 chains the mechanism is already mostly priced out: Friday-afternoon weeklies carry the weekend at ≈ 0.2–0.9 trading-day-equivalents of variance, not the 2 days calendar-time pricing assumes; (iv) the P&L test the hypothesis specifies cannot reach its own gate on our products at any available window — per-weekend noise of 10–18 % of premium against a hypothesised 0.5–1 % effect means a lower bound of +0.3 % needs a measured gap of ≥ 1.5–1.8 % of premium even on the full 2017–2026 pull; and (v) the data the hypothesis says are "already cached" cannot run it: the 0DTE and SPXW caches hold same-day expirations with no overnight content, and the S03 weekly cache, being a stratified sample of sessions, contains 32 usable Friday→Monday pairs for SPY (2017–2022) and none for QQQ or IWM. Expected size after costs: as an entry-timing convention on SPY/QQQ, 0 to perhaps +0.5 % of premium per weekend avoided, sign unproven on these products; as a close-and-reopen rule, negative (1 % round trip plus fees against an unmeasured decay); on single names an entry-timing convention only; and in any case it attaches to no Compass lane, since none is promoted (`09_final_specification.md`).** Six findings:

1. **The primary paper verifies as cited, and it disclaims the strategy.** Jones & Shemesh (*JF* 73(2) 2018) [PR], Table II: delta-hedged puts +0.03 % per trading day vs −0.82 % per nontrading period, difference −0.86 % (t −11.93); calls +0.03 % vs −0.48 % (−0.51 %, t −5.11); Table III: 59 of 60 delta × maturity portfolios lower over nontrading periods, 54 significant; Table IV: ask-to-bid −0.75 % and bid-to-ask −1.00 % for hedged puts, ≤ 5 %-spread subsample −0.63 % (t −9.24), S&P 500 Index puts −1.07 % (t −4.56) but index calls −0.30 % (t −0.91, insignificant); regular weekends −0.79 % (n 851), midweek holidays −0.32 % (n 44, t −0.83), long weekends −0.71 % (n 122); Figure 1: negative in each of 19 years for the unhedged put/call portfolio, significant in 11, "18 out of 19 years negative for puts and 16 out of 19 for calls" for the hedged portfolios, and "the nontrading effect in the second half of the sample is substantially more volatile and slightly smaller on average". Footnote 17: "none of the calculations … factor in the effects of transaction costs … the returns are not feasible as a stand-alone trading strategy. Instead, they represent the additional return that would result from holding previously written options for another day." Conclusion: "Since bid-ask spreads are often large, it is not feasible to capture the entire nontrading effect in a stand-alone trading strategy based on market orders … For an option buyer, the nontrading return is the benefit from delaying the purchase by one day." The hypothesis's framing as a timing rule is therefore the authors' own, and the authors give no number for what that timing is worth after costs.
2. **The effect is measured from closing midpoints, and the one replication at intraday marks cuts it by 50–80 % — but that replication's own levels are not credible, so the executable magnitude is unknown.** Goyenko & Zhang (May 2020 WP) [WP], Table 10 (S&P 500 single-name equity options, dollar-OI-weighted, delta-hedged, Friday close → Monday close): at 16:00 quotes the nontrading-minus-trading difference is −0.95 % (calls, 2005–2010), −0.92 % (calls, 2011–2018), −0.82 % / −1.05 % (puts) — a clean replication of Jones–Shemesh; at 10:00 quotes the same differences are −0.40 %, −0.21 %, −0.15 %, −0.23 %; Table 11 (2015–2018): calls −1.06 % → −0.30 %, puts −1.07 % → −0.26 %; Tables 12–13 (SPX): 2011–2018 calls −0.96 % (16:15) → −0.72 % (10:00), puts +0.36 % (16:15) → −0.26 % (10:00); 2015–2018 calls −1.40 % → −0.96 %, puts +0.89 % → −0.38 %. Their sentence "becomes economically small or evaporates" overstates their tables: at 10 am the gap is −0.15 to −0.40 % for equity options and −0.26 to −0.96 % for SPX, all with t-statistics above 2 (pooled contract-level, 22-lag Newey–West, not comparable to Jones–Shemesh's daily-portfolio t-statistics). The problem is the levels: at 10 am every weekday's delta-hedged return is −1.2 to −1.9 % per day for both equity and SPX options (≈ −300 % a year), which is irreconcilable with Muravyev–Ni's −0.7 % per day and with Bali–Goyal–Mörke–Weigert's +2 to +6 bp mean half-day returns on Cboe intraday quotes 2004–2021 (§1.5). Either the 10 am OptionMetrics/LiveVol quotes are stale or wide against the deltas used, or the closing quotes are; the paper does not resolve which, and no third source with intraday marks reports the weekend gap at all. What survives all three sources is the direction (closing marks overstate the weekend gap) and nothing about its size.
3. **The instrument is outside every published sample, and on our chains the mechanism is mostly priced out.** Jones–Shemesh's maturity buckets are 11–53, 54–118 and 119–252 trading days (Table III); Goyenko–Zhang and Muravyev–Ni use monthly-cycle S&P 500 and SPX options; none measures a 1–7-DTE contract, which is where normalised theta — and therefore any calendar-time mispricing — would be largest. The stated mechanism is testable without P&L: if dealers priced weeklies in calendar time, the Friday 15:50 contract (7 calendar days, 5 sessions to expiry) would carry 1.4× the implied variance per remaining session of the Monday–Thursday contracts (calendar days = sessions). On the S03 cache [LOCAL, §3.3] the median implied variance per remaining session relative to VIX is, Monday → Thursday → Friday: SPY 0.52 / 0.59 / 0.63 / 0.62 / **0.61**; QQQ 1.07 / 1.15 / 1.05 / 1.27 / **1.26**; IWM 1.21 / 1.28 / 1.30 / 1.52 / **1.34** (n 56–97 sessions per cell, 2017–2026). Friday sits 1.00–1.18× the Monday–Thursday mean (1.10–1.18× Monday alone), against 1.40× under calendar-time pricing and 1.00× under trading-time pricing: the two closed days are priced at ≈ 0.2–0.9 trading-day-equivalents of variance in total, not 2.0. Realised variance over the closed weekend is ≈ 0.07–0.10 of a session (§3.4: weekend close-to-close variance is 0.81–0.88× a weekday's; the 15:50 → 09:35 weekend window carries 1.19–1.26× an ordinary night's variance, and an ordinary night is 34–40 % of a day's). Whatever residual excess decay remains is therefore between zero and roughly a third of what Jones–Shemesh's mechanism would produce, and the same-contract calendar-time IV jumps +16 to +20 % from Friday 15:50 to Monday 09:35 (n 32, SE 1.4 %) against +7 to +9 % over an ordinary night (n 94): the market re-marks weekend time out of the weekly on Monday morning rather than letting it decay at the calendar rate.
4. **The P&L test cannot reach the hypothesis's own gate on our products [LOCAL, §3.2, §3.5].** On the 32 SPY weekend/holiday pairs and 94 weekday pairs in the cache (2017-06 → 2022-01), the delta-hedged 0.45-delta weekly call returns −0.37 % [−4.67, +4.70] over the weekend vs −0.01 % [−3.65, +3.78] on a weekday night; the put −1.45 % [−5.46, +2.80] vs +1.92 % [−1.35, +5.36]; the ATM straddle −1.17 % [−4.34, +2.40] vs +0.40 % [−2.22, +3.30]; differences −0.36 [−6.23, +5.89], −3.36 [−8.80, +2.07], −1.57 [−5.79, +2.91]. The per-night standard deviation is 12–18 % of premium for the single contract and 10–14 % for the straddle. The full 2017-06 → 2026-10 window holds 514 nontrading and 1,838 weekday session pairs per ETF, so the standard error of the difference on a complete pull is ≈ 0.6–0.7 % of premium and the 95 % half-width ≈ 1.2–1.4 %: a lower bound of +0.3 % requires a measured weekend decay of ≥ 1.5–1.8 % of premium, two to three times the hypothesised 0.5–1 %. Extending to 2012-06 (≈ 760 weekends) lowers the requirement to ≈ 1.4 %. Pooling the three ETFs does not help (their overnight returns are the same shock). Jones–Shemesh reach t = −11.9 by averaging ≈ 1,600 puts per day across 500 stocks; an index-ETF series has no cross-section to average over, which is also why their own value-weighted SPX series is only t −4.6 on 19 years and insignificant for calls.
5. **The data the hypothesis calls "already cached" cannot run it.** The 0DTE full chains (SPY/QQQ/IWM/SPXW) and the SPXW cache are same-day expirations: nothing survives the close, so they carry no overnight return at all. The S03 weekly cache is a year-stratified *sample* of sessions (SPY 468, QQQ 294, IWM 294 of 2,353), pulled because the vendor throttled the full pull (`05_results.md` S03); consecutive-session pairs sharing a live expiration exist only where the early strata happened to be contiguous: SPY 35 Friday-type pairs and 94 weekday pairs, all 2017–2022 (32 + 94 usable after the next-session check), **QQQ 0, IWM 0**, and no SPXW weeklies at all. "Testable now" is false; a dedicated Friday+Monday whole-chain pull is required: 2 chain-days × 514 weekends × 3 ETFs ≈ 3,100 whole-chain requests (plus SPXW weeklies and the 2012–2017 extension, ≈ 1,500 more) at 3–6 s each, 2.6–7 login-hours if the vendor does not throttle as it did for S03, during which the stock-EOD backfill S07/S08/S13/S14 wait on stays paused (`07_coverage_report.md` §1).
6. **There is nothing for the rule to attach to, and the arithmetic of the rule is weak even where it applies.** Compass's long-option lanes are all unpromoted (S01–S16b; `09_final_specification.md`: "ship no recommendation until a configuration passes the gates"); the baseline Quick Scalps rule is a 0DTE/60-minute hold that never sees a weekend, and the weekly rule (S03) holds ≤ 3 sessions with no information (+$0.02 vs random). An entry-timing shift (open Monday 09:35 instead of Friday 15:50) is free in spread terms — half-spreads of the 0.45-delta SPY weekly are 0.8–1.1 % of mid at both times [LOCAL] — but its expected value is the unmeasured residual of finding 3 (0 to ≈ 3 % of a 7-DTE contract's premium at the mechanism's upper bound, ≈ 0 at the lower), it forgoes whatever Friday-afternoon information the entry was supposed to carry, and S04/S06 say that information is nil anyway. A close-and-reopen rule pays the SPY/QQQ round trip (≈ 1 % of premium + $1.40; C1) against the same unmeasured decay and is negative on single names at 7–8 % round trips. Jones–Shemesh also report that before *long* weekends part of the decay is already paid on the preceding day (hedged puts −0.53 % and calls −0.37 % on the day before a long weekend vs −0.04 % / −0.07 % before regular weekends), i.e., where the effect is conspicuous, traders front-run it.

What survives: a harmless convention — prefer to open a multi-session long on Monday morning rather than Friday afternoon when the entry is otherwise indifferent — which cannot be validated under GATE-v2 on index-ETF P&L and does not need to be, since it costs nothing. The mechanism diagnostic of finding 3 (Friday's implied variance per remaining session vs the week's) is cheap, precise (n ≈ 90 Fridays gives a ±0.03 interval on the ratio) and worth re-running on a full pull as a monitoring statistic; it is not a lane and not an execution rule.

## 1. The claims as cited versus the primary texts

### 1.1 Jones & Shemesh, "Option Mispricing around Nontrading Periods", *Journal of Finance* 73(2), April 2018, 861–900 [PR]; full text read

http://faculty.marshall.usc.edu/Christopher-Jones/pdf/jones_shemesh_2018_jf.pdf (DOI 10.1111/jofi.12603; Wiley abstract page returned 403). Data: OptionMetrics IvyDB closing bid-ask quotes, options on S&P 500 constituents (as of the prior year end) with five consecutive days of positive volume plus arbitrage, dividend, reversal and spread filters; ≈ 1,635 puts and 2,533 calls per day with an average relative spread of ≈ 14–15 % (Table I); January 4, 1996 – August 28, 2014; returns from closing midpoints, excess of the riskless rate with calendar-day accrual; portfolios weighted by lagged dollar open interest; Black–Scholes deltas from IvyDB implied volatilities (calendar-time convention); the "nontrading period" is any interval between closes longer than one calendar day (regular weekends, long weekends, midweek holidays). Standard errors: Newey–West with 22 lags on a regression on a nontrading dummy; block-bootstrap p-values "very close".

Every number in the hypothesis checks: Table II (hedged puts +0.03 % / −0.82 %, −0.86 %, t −11.93; hedged calls +0.03 % / −0.48 %, −0.51 %, t −5.11; Monday-ending hedged-put return −0.85 %, Tuesday −0.16 %, Wednesday +0.19 %, Thursday +0.25 %, Friday −0.23 %; no nontrading effect in the stock hedge portfolios); Table III (59/60, 54 significant; 11–53-day options with −0.25 > δ ≥ −0.50: −1.58 %, with −0.10 > δ ≥ −0.25: −2.73 %, t −10.21; 0.01 < δ ≤ 0.10 calls −4.36 %, t −5.56; 119–252-day options one-third to one-quarter as large); Table IV (ask-to-bid −0.75 %, bid-to-ask −1.00 %; equal-weighted −2.13 %; options on all stocks −0.61 %; zero-volume contracts −0.42 %; five days of volume > 100 contracts −1.12 %; ≤ 5 % spreads −0.63 %, t −9.24; SPX value-weighted puts −1.07 % t −4.56, calls −0.30 % t −0.91, equal-weighted deep-OTM −4.71 %; regular weekends / midweek holidays / long weekends −0.79 / −0.32 / −0.71 % with n 851 / 44 / 122). Table V: the one-day decline in implied variance over a nontrading period exceeds realised variance by b ≈ 0.0006–0.0009 against an average stock-level daily realised variance of 0.00082 — "option prices embed a full extra day of variance over each nontrading period"; for the S&P 500 Index the coefficient is smaller in absolute terms but at least as large relative to its realised variance. Section III: the effect loads on gamma/theta (76 % of the variation in average returns attributable to normalised theta) and not on vega; no risk measure "varies enough between trading and nontrading periods to explain the differences"; the authors' reading is French (1984) calendar-time mispricing, and they estimate the transfer at "close to $1.5 billion over the course of a single weekend".

Three things the hypothesis summary does not carry. (a) **Costs:** the two disclaimers quoted in finding 1, plus the Muravyev–Pearson caveat that algorithmic liquidity takers "pay costs only one-fifth as large as those implied by the bid-ask quotes" — the retail marketable order pays the quoted column (sibling note `academic-anomalies.md` §10). (b) **Attenuation inside the sample:** the second half is "substantially more volatile and slightly smaller on average"; the authors attribute part of it to algorithmic market making after the 2003 Options Linkage and note only that "the last year of the sample displays an above-average effect". (c) **Scope:** no 1–7-DTE contracts (the lowest maturity bucket starts at 11 trading days), no ETF options, index calls insignificant, closing quotes throughout, and an SPX sample the authors call "statistically weaker" (footnote 10).

Evidence-quality reading: a peer-reviewed, large-sample, internally robust result with a stated mechanism and 19-year consistency — the strongest kind of anomaly paper — whose measurement convention (closing midpoints) was challenged two years later (§1.3) and whose sample ends in 2014, before SPX overnight trading (2015), the explosion of weeklies and the retail/wholesaler regime (Bryzgalova et al.; sibling note §4).

### 1.2 Muravyev & Ni, "Why do option returns change sign from day to night?", *JFE* 136(1), 2020, 219–238 [PR]; abstract only

https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v136y2020i1p219-238.html ; Semantic Scholar record for DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2018.12.006 (open-access pointer: SSRN 2820264, which returned 403). Abstract (paraphrased by the fetch tool; the IDEAS page carries it): delta-hedged S&P 500 index option returns average about −0.7 % per day; close-to-open about −1 % per day, open-to-close about +0.3 %; "similar return pattern holds for all maturity and moneyness categories and equity options"; the authors argue option prices do not fully reflect that volatility is much higher intraday than overnight. The sample period (January 2004 – April 2013, intraday bid-ask quotes) and the equity-option magnitudes (−0.4 % overnight, +0.1 % intraday) in the hypothesis come from the CXO summary cited in the sibling note [SEC] and were not verified here. The paper measures weekday overnight periods pooled with weekends; it is a daily-frequency relative of Jones–Shemesh and shares the closing-mark exposure (Goyenko–Zhang re-estimate it and find the intraday sign flips in 2015–2018 and becomes "statistically indistinguishable from zero" with inventory controls; sibling note §6). Bali et al. (§1.5) find the opposite signs after 2012 on Cboe intraday data. It is cited here as supporting the existence of a night/day asymmetry in 2004–2013 SPX data, not as evidence about weekends specifically or about any post-2015 magnitude.

### 1.3 Goyenko & Zhang, "Price Pressures and Noise in Option Returns", McGill WP, 24 May 2020 [WP]; full text read

https://ruslangoyenko.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Option-Returns-MAY-24-2020.pdf. Data: LiveVol intraday quotes and trades matched to OptionMetrics (deltas, open interest), S&P 500 constituents and SPX, 2005–2010 and 2011–2018 with a 2015–2018 "SPX night trading" sub-sample; delta-hedged returns from 10:00, 11:00, 12:00, 13:00, 14:00, 15:00 and 16:00 (16:15 for SPX) mid-quotes day t to day t+1; filters |δ| ∈ [0.02, 0.98], dollar spread ≤ $3, mid ≥ $0.50, effective relative spread ≤ 70 %; weekend tables retain only contracts traded all five days, Friday close → Monday close, as in Jones–Shemesh. Publication status could not be verified (no journal record found by direct URL); treated as a working paper.

The weekend tables are reproduced in finding 2. Two further points. First, the mechanism they offer is different from Jones–Shemesh's: closing midpoints are pushed by market makers' overnight inventory management (quoted spreads "jump by 50 bps at the closing"), and "the negative weekend effect is rather attributed to the end-of-day noise caused by price pressures" (p. 25). Second, their own post-2015 result at closing marks is the reverse anomaly: with weekends removed, 16:00-marked delta-hedged equity calls earn +17 bp and puts +11 bp per day in 2015–2018 (−17 bp for puts in 2011–2014), and SPX puts' weekend-minus-weekday difference at 16:15 flips to +0.89 % in 2015–2018 while being −0.95 % at 16:00 fifteen minutes earlier (Table 13 and p. 26) — their evidence that closing marks are not prices at which anything is bought. For the hypothesis, the usable content is: (a) the closing-mark weekend gap replicates through 2018; (b) at 10 am the gap is −0.15 to −0.40 % (equity) and −0.26 to −0.96 % (SPX) — still negative, not zero — and (c) the 10 am daily levels (−1.2 to −1.9 % per day, every weekday) are not economically interpretable (see §1.5), so neither the 16:00 nor the 10:00 magnitude can be taken as the executable size.

### 1.4 Papagelis & Dotsis, "The Variance Risk Premium Over Trading and Nontrading Periods", *Journal of Futures Markets* 2025, DOI 10.1002/fut.22589 (SSRN 4954623) [PR]; abstract excerpt only

Semantic Scholar record (via the Jones–Shemesh citation list): the paper splits the variance risk premium into overnight and intraday components using model-free implied-variance indices for the US, Europe and Asia; "the VRP is significantly negative [overnight], whereas during the intraday trading period, the VRP becomes positive"; the intraday component predicts equity returns at 1–3-month horizons, the overnight component at 6–12 months. The Harbourfront Substack summary [SEC] — https://harbourfrontquant.substack.com/p/breaking-down-the-volatility-risk — adds that a long-volatility position opened at the open and closed at the close "tends to be profitable on most days, except Fridays"; this sentence is secondary, unquantified and unverified. The paper is about variance-swap P&L on indices, not option returns at quotes, and gives no weekend number in any text reachable here. It supports a sign (overnight variance is paid for; intraday is not) and nothing about size or feasibility.

### 1.5 Bali, Goyal, Mörke & Weigert, "In Search of Seasonality in Intraday and Overnight Option Returns", CFR WP 26-02, December 2025 [WP]; full text read

https://cfr-cologne.de/download/workingpaper/cfr-26-02.pdf. Cboe intraday quotes, trades and Greeks for US equity options, January 2004 – December 2021; the "open" is the 10:00 mid-quote, the overnight is 16:00 → 10:00, the intraday 10:00 → 16:00, ATM options, delta re-hedged every 30 minutes. The paper is not about the weekend: it documents half-day momentum and reversal of 0.22–0.45 % per half-day (gross) and tests weekdays only as a robustness check — "To ensure our findings are not driven by mispricing related to non-trading periods, such as the weekend effects in Jones and Shemesh (2018) … both the momentum and reversal patterns are remarkably stable and economically large across all five days" (Table E.3). Three facts in it bear on L6: (a) average half-day delta-hedged returns are "slightly positive, ranging between 2 and 6 basis points per half-day period", medians −23 bp overnight and −20 bp intraday — an order of magnitude below Goyenko–Zhang's −1.2 to −1.9 % per day at 10 am marks on overlapping years, which is why the 10 am magnitudes of §1.3 cannot be taken at face value; (b) Figure B.1 (three-year rolling medians): "the average intraday median option return is positive until 2012, it turns negative for the rest of the sample. In contrast, the overnight average median return is lowest … around 2010 and 2012 and starts to increase until the rest of the sample" — the Muravyev–Ni night/day asymmetry attenuates and reverses after 2012 in these data; (c) the authors state their returns are gross and make no implementability claim ("Even if our strategy's alpha is zero after costs"). The sibling note's reading (not tradeable at retail round trips) stands.

### 1.6 Later literature citing Jones–Shemesh and Muravyev–Ni (Semantic Scholar citation lists; titles and abstract excerpts only)

Eighteen papers cite Jones–Shemesh in the Semantic Scholar graph; none is a US replication of the weekend effect, none uses weeklies or ETF options, and none mentions weekends or holidays in its title. The day/night strand continues in emerging markets with *opposite* signs to the US pattern: "The asymmetry in day and night option returns: Evidence from an emerging market" (*JFM* 2024, DOI 10.1002/fut.22512, Nifty options: "Positive and significant overnight option returns are accompanied by negative intraday returns") and "Asymmetric Option Returns in China" (*JFM* 2026, DOI 10.1002/fut.70095: "call (put) returns are positive (negative) overnight but negative (positive) intraday", attributed to retail gambling and insurance demand). "On the Nature of (Jump) Skewness Risk Premia" (*Management Science* 2023, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2023.4734) finds "the skewness premium is higher when markets are closed than during trading hours" — consistent with closed periods carrying a priced premium, which is a risk reading, not a mispricing reading. "Option pricing with overnight and intraday volatility" (*JFM* 2023, DOI 10.1002/fut.22448) was not retrievable. Net: the nontrading sign is reproduced in several markets at closing marks, its sign is market-dependent, and no paper reachable here measures it after 2018 in US data at executable quotes.

## 2. Evidence-quality reading across the sources

- **Real in-sample, not a data-mining artefact.** One pre-registered-looking hypothesis (French 1984), one sample, consistent across 19 years, 59/60 portfolios, both signs of option, index and single names, replicated at 16:00 marks on 2005–2018 by an unrelated team (Goyenko–Zhang Table 10). This is not a cross-sectional sort mined from hundreds of characteristics.
- **But partly a measurement artefact, by the only replication with intraday marks**, and the size at intraday marks is contradicted between sources (G–Z's −1.2 to −1.9 % per day at 10 am vs Bali et al.'s +2 to +6 bp mean half-day returns on Cboe quotes vs Muravyev–Ni's −0.7 % per day). The honest statement is: the weekend-minus-weekday gap at the close is ≈ −0.8 to −1.1 % of premium for liquid S&P 500 single-name options through 2018; away from the close it is smaller by a factor of two to five; no source gives a number for a quote at which a retail order would fill.
- **Attenuating and regime-dependent.** Jones–Shemesh: smaller and noisier after 2005. Goyenko–Zhang: closing-mark weekday returns turn positive after 2015 and the SPX-put weekend gap flips sign at 16:15. Bali et al.: the night/day asymmetry reverses after 2012 in Cboe data. Emerging markets: opposite sign. None of this says the weekend effect is gone; all of it says its post-2015 size in US index products is unmeasured.
- **Mechanism-specific, and the mechanism is a desk-practice error that has an obvious fix.** Calendar-time variance over weekends is a known mistake (French 1984; Whaley's own 2000 VXO note quoted by Jones–Shemesh); weighting weekend days at a fraction of a trading day in the vol-time clock is standard practice for anyone quoting short-dated options. The local diagnostic (§3.3) shows exactly that in 2017–2026 index-ETF weeklies: Friday's contract is priced with the weekend at ≈ 0.2–0.9 trading-day-equivalents, between the calendar (2.0) and trading-time (0) conventions, which leaves at most a fraction of the published effect in the instrument the hypothesis would trade.
- **Instrument mismatch.** Published: monthly-cycle single-name and SPX options, 11–252 trading days, closing quotes, 1996–2018. Proposed: 1–7-DTE SPY/QQQ/IWM/SPXW weeklies, 15:50 and 09:35 NBBO, 2017–2026. The extrapolation runs through normalised theta (larger for weeklies, so the effect "should" be larger) and through dealer practice (weeklies are where the weekend convention is most carefully handled, so it "should" be smaller); the data say the second dominates.
- **Multiplicity:** low in the paper; the hypothesis's own proposed cells (2 contracts × 2 marks × 2 fill conventions × 4 products × years) are many, and A5's batch rule would apply.

### 3.3 Calendar-time diagnostic across all cached sessions (Part B)

At 15:50 on every cached session, the nearest-Friday weekly's ATM implied variance to expiry (calendar time), divided by the VIX's variance per session ((VIX/100)²/252) and by the number of sessions remaining to expiry. If the weekend were priced at calendar weight, the Friday value would be 7/5 = 1.40× the Monday–Thursday values (whose calendar days equal their sessions); at trading-time weight, 1.00×. Medians (n per cell):

| | Mon (4 d / 4 s) | Tue (3/3) | Wed (2/2) | Thu (1/1) | **Fri (7 d / 5 s)** | Fri ÷ Mon–Thu mean | Fri ÷ Mon | implied weekend weight (sessions) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SPY (n 96/85/97/96/89) | 0.521 | 0.589 | 0.628 | 0.622 | **0.612** | 1.04 | 1.17 | 0.2 – 0.9 |
| QQQ (59/62/59/56/57) | 1.069 | 1.149 | 1.048 | 1.271 | **1.260** | 1.11 | 1.18 | 0.6 – 0.9 |
| IWM (59/62/59/56/57) | 1.211 | 1.282 | 1.300 | 1.518 | **1.337** | 1.01 | 1.10 | 0.0 – 0.5 |

(Implied weekend weight = 5 × (ratio − 1); the Monday–Thursday ramp — rising implied variance per session as expiry nears — is the usual short-dated premium and makes the Monday-only comparison an upper bound.) Against calendar-time pricing's 2.0 sessions and a realised ≈ 0.1 (§3.4), index-ETF weeklies in 2017–2026 carry the weekend at 0–0.9 session-equivalents. The residual excess over realised (0–0.8 sessions of ≈ 5.9 total on a Friday 7-DTE contract) bounds the expected weekend decay of the ATM straddle at mid between 0 and ≈ 3 % of premium (price ∝ √variance; half of 0–13 % of variance); the point estimate against the week's mean is ≈ 0–1 % for SPY and IWM and ≈ 2 % for QQQ. These are mechanism bounds, not measured returns, and the 3.2 intervals contain all of them.

### 3.4 Realised variance of the closures (script `realized_var.py`)

SPY/QQQ/IWM minute bars, 2017-06 → 2026-10, log returns: weekend-or-holiday close-to-close variance is 0.81 / 0.88 / 0.83 × a weekday's (n 411–480 vs 1,828; French–Roll's finding reproduced); the 15:50 → 09:35 window carries 1.26 / 1.24 / 1.19 × an ordinary night's variance; an ordinary night is 36 / 34 / 40 % of close-to-close variance. The closed weekend therefore adds ≈ 0.07–0.10 of a session's variance beyond an ordinary overnight, against the 2.0 sessions of calendar time and the 0–0.9 priced in §3.3.

### 3.5 Power against the hypothesis's own gate

Per-pair standard deviation of the hedged overnight return: 12–18 % of premium (single 0.45δ weekly), 10–14 % (ATM straddle). Full 2017–2026 pull, one ETF: SE(difference) ≈ 14 % × √(1/514 + 1/1838) ≈ 0.70 % (single), ≈ 0.60 % (straddle); 95 % half-width ≈ 1.4 % / 1.2 %; a positive lower bound ≥ 0.3 % needs a measured weekend-minus-weekday gap ≥ 1.7 % / 1.5 % of premium. With 2012–2026 (≈ 760 nontrading pairs): ≈ 1.4 % / 1.3 %. The three ETFs share the overnight shock, so pooling them lowers the SE by far less than √3. The 10:00-mark variant (Goyenko–Zhang's robustness column) adds a sixth hour of SPY drift to the window and raises the noise. A single-name panel (Tier A, 34 names with complete bars) would average idiosyncratic noise the way Jones–Shemesh do, but C1 half-spreads of 3.5–4.1 % per side put every single-name expression under water regardless of the result.

### 3.6 Cost of the test

Friday + Monday whole-chain 1-minute NBBO for the nearest 7-DTE weekly, SPY/QQQ/IWM 2017-06 → 2026-10: ≈ 3,100 requests (3–6 s each; the S03 pull was throttled to a stratified sample), plus SPXW weeklies (≈ 1,000) and 2012–2017 (≈ 1,500): 2.6–7 login-hours on the single ThetaData login, competing with the S11 event pull and the 2022 → 2012 stock-EOD backfill (`07_coverage_report.md` §1). The outcome, at best, is a timing convention for lanes that do not exist.

## 4. Expected size after costs

- **Entry-delay convention, SPY/QQQ weeklies (buy Monday 09:35 instead of Friday 15:50):** spread-neutral (half-spreads 0.8–1.1 % at both times); expected gain = the residual weekend decay at mid, 0 to ≈ 3 % of a 7-DTE contract's premium by the mechanism bound, ≈ 0–1 % at the point estimates of §3.3 for SPY/IWM, measured −0.4 % (call) / −3.4 % (put) ± 5–6 % on 32 weekends. Call it 0 to +0.5 % of premium per weekend avoided, sign unproven; it forgoes the Friday-afternoon thesis, which S04/S06 value at nil.
- **Close Friday, reopen Monday (SPY/QQQ):** pays ≈ 1 % of premium + $1.40 against the same unmeasured decay: negative in expectation at the point estimates, breakeven at the mechanism's upper bound.
- **Single names:** entry-timing only; any close-and-reopen pays 7–8 % of premium (C1).
- **SPXW weeklies:** no cache, same arithmetic at a 0.9 % half-spread; SPX calls were insignificant in Jones–Shemesh and the SPX-put gap flipped sign at 16:15 in 2015–2018 (Goyenko–Zhang).
- **Short-premium mirror (write Friday, cover Monday):** outside the Compass long-option scope (`08_port_spec_3abida.md` §5); H17 found weekday skips lower the mean for the 0DTE carry grids, and the 0DTE straddle cannot be held over a weekend; a weekly short straddle over the weekend is a new carry hypothesis with GATE-v2 tail gates, not this one.

## 5. What would change the verdict

1. A full Friday/Monday weekly-chain pull (SPY/QQQ/IWM, 2012-06 → 2026-10) in which the hedged weekend-minus-weekday gap at the NBBO mid has a lower bound ≥ +0.3 % of premium — which, per §3.5, requires a measured gap ≥ 1.4–1.7 % — **and** the §3.3 Friday ratio ≥ 1.3 (weekend priced at ≥ 1.5 sessions). The second condition is the cheap one and is already failing on the cached sample (1.01–1.18).
2. A peer-reviewed post-2018 US replication at executable intraday quotes on short-dated index products. None was found in the citation graph.
3. A Compass lane that holds positions over weekends and has passed GATE-v2; the rule has no object until then. If one appears, the convention of §0 can be attached to it without a test, because it costs nothing.
