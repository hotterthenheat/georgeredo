# Academic option-return anomalies: what a retail account could in principle trade (2026-10-14)

Companion to `research/compass/registry.jsonl` (closed ids cited by name), `02_coverage_matrix.md` (data floors) and `05_results.md` (S01–S16b). Grading legend used throughout: **[PR]** peer-reviewed journal; **[WP]** working paper (SSRN/NBER/CFR/Fed, conference version); **[PRAC]** practitioner white paper (AQR, exchange); **[SEC]** secondary summary (CXO Advisory, Quantpedia, Alpha Architect, newsletters) — never evidence, used only where the primary text could not be retrieved, and labelled as such; **[VM]** vendor marketing — none of the sources below is vendor marketing; where a commercial site appears it is only for a data-path (macroption) and is flagged. Every number below is either read from the primary PDF (held in the session scratchpad, converted with `pdftotext`) or quoted from the publisher abstract; where only a secondary source gave the number this is said in the sentence.

Cost model for every "after-cost" statement: marketable orders at the NBBO (pay the full quoted half-spread each side), $0.70 per contract per side, no rebates; measured half-spreads from registry `C1`: SPY/QQQ 0.5 % of mid, IWM 0.7–1.0 %, SPXW 0.9 %, single names 3.5–4.1 % median (CRML 25–43 %); 0DTE straddle buy-back of near-worthless legs 3.3 % median of credit. Two cross-cutting findings that change how every paper below should be read are stated first.

## 0. Two results that discount the whole literature for a 2026 retail account

**0.1 The index option risk premium that most of these papers were written on has shrunk toward zero since about 2010.** Dew-Becker & Giglio, "The Decline of the Variance Risk Premium: Evidence from Traded and Synthetic Options", Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago WP 2025-17, 4 Sep 2025 [WP] — https://www.chicagofed.org/-/media/publications/working-papers/2025/wp2025-17.pdf (also SSRN 5525882). Abstract: "Equity index options historically displayed sharply negative returns and CAPM alphas … over the past 15 years, option alphas have become indistinguishable from zero." Section 4.1: rolling 10-year information ratios for traded 5 %-OTM SPX puts "were strongly negative until 2009, when they begin to trend up, eventually turning starkly positive in 2020"; the rolling 10-year information ratios of delta-hedged 5 % OTM puts and of ATM straddles "have risen over time, converging to zero in 2020 (with a complete convergence for the ATM straddles coming even before Covid)"; "as of January, 2020, the difference in rolling information ratios … is no longer significant"; the RV-minus-VIX² proxy for a variance swap behaves the same. Their reading: "the premium is no larger than what would be expected from the CAPM beta … the alpha contribution has shrunk." Sample 1987–2022 (traded), 1926–2022 (synthetic). This is the single most important fact for the short-premium side of the sweep: the unconditional "sell index volatility" edge of Coval–Shumway (2001), Bakshi–Kapadia (2003) and Bondarenko (2014) is a pre-2010 phenomenon in the data, which is exactly what H9/H11/H13/S15 found on 2022–2026 0DTE chains.

**0.2 Most published option-return anomalies are measured from end-of-day closing midpoints, which are biased.** Goyenko & Zhang, "Price Pressures and Noise in Option Returns", McGill WP, May 2020 [WP] — https://ruslangoyenko.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Option-Returns-MAY-24-2020.pdf. "Delta-hedged option and straddle returns on S&P500 Index and equity options computed using end-of-day closing prices are biased upward. The bias can reach more than 100 bps per day and is attributed to overnight inventory risk." Daily delta-hedged and straddle returns computed from 10 am, 11 am or 12 pm mid-quotes are "more than 100 bps, 90 bps, or 70 bps respectively lower" than those from the 4 pm (equity) / 4:15 pm (SPX) close; about 80 % of option net order imbalance and volume occurs before noon; the closing quoted spread "jump[s] by 50 bps at the closing compared to the rest of the day". Sample: S&P 500 components, 2005–2010 and 2011–2018. Implications they draw: Muravyev–Ni's positive intraday return and Jones–Shemesh's weekend effect shrink or "evaporate" with 10 am quotes (2015–2018 sub-sample); straddle returns at 10 am are −1.16 % per day for both equity and SPX options. For us: every replication below should mark positions at 10:00–11:00 ThetaData 1-minute NBBO, not at the close; this is a data advantage over the literature, not a handicap.

## 1. Variance risk premium: level and timing

### 1.1 Level (why the unconditional short-vol lanes failed)

- Coval & Shumway, "Expected Option Returns", *Journal of Finance* 56(3), 2001, 983–1009 [PR] — https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/455dbe73-b56b-44f3-841f-c7751deffc7a. Abstract: "zero-beta, at-the-money straddle positions produce average losses of approximately three percent per week". Index straddles; returns computed from the first quote after 10 am ET (which is why Goyenko–Zhang cite it approvingly). Costs: mid-quote returns, no cost analysis in the abstract.
- Bakshi & Kapadia, "Delta-Hedged Gains and the Negative Market Volatility Risk Premium", *RFS* 16(2), 2003, 527–566 [PR] — https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v16y2003i2p527-566.html. S&P 500 delta-hedged long option positions underperform zero; shortfall smaller OTM, larger when volatility is high. No cost analysis.
- Bondarenko, "Why Are Put Options So Expensive?", *Quarterly Journal of Finance* 4(3), 2014 [PR] — https://ideas.repec.org/a/wsi/qjfxxx/v04y2014i03ns2010139214500153.html. Model-free rejection of all models in a broad class. Magnitudes (one-month unhedged S&P 500 puts: ATM 39 %/month, deep-OTM 95 %/month; Jensen's alpha of selling ATM puts ≈ 23 %/month) are from CXO's summary of the 2004 draft [SEC] — https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=1415 — not verified against the published text. Broadie–Chernov–Johannes cite Bondarenko (2003) ATM put returns of −40 %/month and deep-OTM −95 %/month.
- Chambers, Foy, Liebner & Lu, "Index Option Returns: Still Puzzling", *RFS* 27(6), 2014, 1915–1928 [PR] — https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v27y2014i6p1915-1928..html. 1987–2012: "generally possible to reject the hypothesis that put returns are consistent with option pricing models"; "the average cost of buying out-of-the-money put options to provide tail-risk protection to a portfolio may include a significant premium."
- Dew-Becker, Giglio, Le & Rodriguez, "The Price of Variance Risk", *JFE* 123(2), 2017, 225–250; NBER WP 21182 (May 2015) read here [PR, WP] — https://www.nber.org/papers/w21182.pdf. Variance swaps 1996–2014: annual Sharpe ratios of zero-coupon variance claims "are negative for the one- and two-month claims (at −1.4 and −0.5, respectively), but all other Sharpe ratios" ≈ 0; a pure realized-variance shock is priced at an annualized Sharpe of −2.72; investors "indifferent to news about future variance at horizons ranging from 1 month to 14 years". Dealer bid-ask on variance swaps 1–2 % of strike up to one year. So whatever short-vol premium exists lives in the ≤ 2-month tenor — the tenor a retail SPY/SPX short straddle or strangle occupies — and is the tenor Dew-Becker & Giglio (0.1) show converging to zero after 2010.
- Israelov & Nielsen, "Still Not Cheap: Portfolio Protection in Calm Markets", AQR / *JPM* 41(4), 2015 [PRAC] — https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/Still-Not-Cheap-Portfolio-Protection-in-Calm-Markets. "It is the volatility risk premium … and not the absolute level of implied volatility, that determines whether put option portfolio protection is cheap or expensive." Israelov & Tummala, "Which Index Options Should You Sell?", AQR WP, June 2017 [PRAC] — https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Working-Paper/Which-Index-Options-Should-You-Sell: delta-hedged S&P 500 options 1996–2015, "the most compensated options to sell per unit of stress-test loss are front-month options with strikes near-the-money and moderately below the index level." Both pre-date the post-2010 convergence being measurable and both are house research from a volatility-selling manager; treat as hypotheses, not evidence.

Registry: H9, H11, H13 (0DTE ATM straddles at measured NBBO: rejected), S15 (unconditional 10:00 0DTE straddle −$15.46 [−32.68, +0.29], n 1,941), H15 (14:05 SPXW strangle carry lane: open, forward paper only), H16 (win rate is a consequence of strike distance), H17 (FOMC + month-end skip; VIX-level skips lower the mean). Nothing in 1.1 reopens them; Dew-Becker & Giglio explain them.

### 1.2 Timing: implied-vs-realized, VIX term structure, VIX premium

- Bollerslev, Tauchen & Zhou, "Expected Stock Returns and Variance Risk Premia", *RFS* 22(11), 2009, 4463–4492 [PR]; FEDS 2007-11 draft read here [WP] — https://www.federalreserve.gov/Pubs/Feds/2007/200711/200711pap.pdf. Draft: implied-minus-realized variance "is able to explain more than fifteen percent of the ex-post time series variation in quarterly excess returns on the market portfolio over the 1990 to 2005 sample period"; with P/E, R² 26.37 %; the published abstract says only "a nontrivial fraction … particularly strong at the intermediate quarterly return horizon". This predicts the *stock market*, not option returns, at a quarterly horizon; it is a stock-timing result and is not a long-option lane (a 3-month SPY call spends ~4–5 % of spot in premium to collect the few hundred bp the VRP predicts).
- Johnson, "Risk Premia and the VIX Term Structure", *JFQA* 52(6), 2017, 2461–2490 [PR] — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/risk-premia-and-the-vix-term-structure/56572D1F060448571BD8F597C732D9C3. Abstract: the shape of the VIX term structure reflects the price of variance risk, not expected VIX changes; its second principal component (SLOPE) "predicts the excess returns of synthetic S&P 500 variance swaps, VIX futures, and S&P 500 straddles for all maturities", incremental to other VRP proxies. Sample 1996–2013 and the sign (low slope → high subsequent returns on variance assets) are from CXO's summary [SEC] — https://www.cxoadvisory.com/volatility-effects/vix-term-structure-slope-and-variance-asset-future-returns/; the 2011 job-market draft on the Bauer site is the equity-premium version, not this paper. Magnitudes for the straddle regressions were not retrieved. **This is the one VRP-timing claim that is stated directly on an instrument a retail account trades (SPX straddles).**
- Cheng, "The VIX Premium", *RFS* 32(1), 2019, 180–227 [PR] — https://econbiz.de/Record/the-vix-premium-cheng-ing-haw/10012033380. The ex-ante VIX futures premium "fall[s] or stay[s] flat when ex ante measures of risk rise"; premiums predict ex-post VIX futures returns "with a coefficient near one"; falling premiums predict higher subsequent risk. Instrument is VIX futures (futures account; institutional/futures lane for our purposes).
- Simon & Campasano, "The VIX Futures Basis: Evidence and Trading Strategies", EFMA 2013 conference version [WP]; a 2014 *Journal of Derivatives* publication is reported by Quantpedia but was not verified here — https://quantpedia.com/strategies/exploiting-term-structure-of-vix-futures [SEC]. Short the front VIX future in contango (daily roll > 0.10), hedged with ES, five-day hold, 2006–2011; "highly profitable and robust to transaction costs" per the draft. VIX futures, not options: not retail-options-accessible under this brief.

Data for 1.2 that we did not have in the coverage matrix: Cboe publishes the VIX family as free daily CSVs with no key (verified today by direct download, redirect to cdn-api.cboe.com): VIX from 1990-01-02, VIX6M from 2008-01-02, VIX3M from 2009-09-18, VIX9D from 2011-01-04, VIX1D from 2022-05-13, all through 2026-10-13 (`https://cdn.cboe.com/api/global/us_indices/daily_prices/{VIX,VIX9D,VIX3M,VIX6M,VIX1D}_History.csv`). This lifts the 2017-01 index-regime floor in `02_coverage_matrix.md` §1 for daily-resolution regime variables over the whole 2012-06 → option-NBBO window; the term-structure slope (VIX3M − VIX9D, VIX6M − VIX) is available daily from 2011.

Registry: S15 tested the *level* condition (straddle-implied move ÷ trailing realized) on 0DTE and found the wrong sign (rich implied → larger realized). H17 tested VIX-*level* skips for carry (they lower the mean). Neither tested the term-structure *slope* nor the 30-day tenor where Dew-Becker et al. place the premium. **L1 below is new.**

**L1 (proposed screen, low prior).** SPY (and SPXW) 30-DTE ATM straddle and ±0.5×expected-move strangle, opened monthly at 10:30 (not the close; 0.2), held 21 sessions or to expiry, 2012-06 → 2026-10 (≈ 172 months, every month, no selection), both long and short, cells by quintile of the VIX3M − VIX9D slope and of VIX − trailing-21-day realized, fills at the ThetaData 1-minute NBBO, C1 costs. Cost on SPY: a 30-DTE ATM straddle is ≈ 4 % of spot ≈ $26 per share on a $650 SPY; two legs × two sides × 0.5 % half-spread ≈ 2.0 % of premium ≈ $52 per straddle, plus $2.80 fees: ≈ 2.1 % of premium per round trip, ≈ 8 bp of notional. The signal therefore needs to move expected straddle P&L by more than ≈ 2 % of premium per month to show after costs. Expected: unconditional short ≈ 0 since 2010 (0.1); Johnson's slope effect is "economically significant" on 1996–2013 data that is mostly pre-convergence; a positive day-clustered lower bound in any slope quintile after 2012 is unlikely but the test is cheap (option EOD for SPY 2012 → is in the vendor floor; the monthly straddle needs ~2 × 172 contract histories). If the long-straddle cell on inverted slopes clears zero it would be the first long-option carry-timing result; if the short cell does, it belongs with H15 as a carry lane subject to GATE-v2 tail gates (±10 %/±15 % shock losses per lot were −$65k/−$99k for the SPXW strangle).

## 2. Option momentum and reversal

Heston, Jones, Khorram, Li & Mo, "Option Momentum", *Journal of Finance* 78(6), 2023, 3141–3192 [PR]; September 2021 draft read here — https://www.acem.sjtu.edu.cn/sffs/2021w/pdfs/3.pdf (published DOI 10.1111/jofi.13279). Monthly returns on zero-delta ATM straddles on individual equities, OptionMetrics, 282 months from January 1996, ≈ 1,369 straddles per month. Table 2 (quintiles, mean monthly return, Newey-West t): formation lags 2–12 (classic momentum) high-minus-low **+6.22 % per month (t 7.97)**; lags 1–1 (one-month reversal) **−4.09 % (t −6.18)**; lags 13–24 +3.14 % (t 3.99); lags 25–36 +2.61 % (t 3.35); 12-month momentum annualized Sharpe 1.53; "significant in every five-year subsample"; no long-run reversal. **Every quintile portfolio of long straddles has a negative mean**: the best (2–12 momentum, high quintile) is −1.55 % per month (t −0.85); the IV−HV low quintile (Goyal–Saretto's cheap-volatility leg) is −1.86 % (t −1.10); the IV-term-spread high quintile (Vasquez's leg) −2.78 % (t −1.69). The long-short profit is the short leg. Table 13 (after costs, one-way cost = fraction of quoted half-spread following Muravyev–Pearson: "algo" 20.3 %, "adjusted" 51.6 %, "effective" 75.8 %, "quoted" 100 %): momentum main sample 6.22 % → 3.62 % (algo) → −0.40 % (adjusted) → −3.56 % (effective) → **−6.78 % (quoted)**; "low-cost with deciles" (spreads < 10 % of mid, extreme deciles) 7.23 → 5.99 → 4.08 → 2.60 % (t 1.75) → **1.12 % (t 0.75)** with zero exercise costs, and 0.88 % (t 0.58) / −0.61 % with stock-liquidation costs; reversal low-cost deciles 1.99 % (t 1.64) at the quoted spread; composite (momentum + reversal z-scores) low-cost deciles 1.98 % (t 1.47) at quoted, −0.30 % with exercise costs. SPAN-style margin "shrink[s] [returns] by 50 %–55 %". Authors' own summary: "Average option momentum returns are close to zero after paying the full bid-ask spread for options with below-median bid-ask spreads." A retail marketable order pays the quoted column. Retail accessibility: the short leg is naked single-name straddle writing (highest approval tier, Reg-T margin); the long leg alone is negative; defined-risk substitutes (iron butterflies) pay four spreads (S10-rerun showed verticals lose two spreads). Registry: S14 is *stock* 12-1 momentum (different asset; underpowered on 2024–2026); option momentum is not closed. Testable with our data: yes — ThetaData option EOD + OI from 2012-06 and 1-minute NBBO for 10:00 marks; needs monthly ATM straddle histories for ~800–1,700 names × 170 months (per-root-per-month EOD pulls; a Tier A subset of ~150 roots is a tractable first pass). Expected after retail costs: ≈ 0 to +1 % per month before margin for the low-spread-decile composite, with t < 1.6 in the paper; the only long-only use is as a *ranking* of which straddles lose least.

Bali, Goyal, Mörke & Weigert, "In Search of Seasonality in Intraday and Overnight Option Returns", CFR WP 26-02, December 2025 [WP] — https://cfr-cologne.de/download/workingpaper/cfr-26-02.pdf. Half-day delta-hedged option returns (10:00 and close marks; OptionMetrics + intraday data to 2021): same-period momentum (intraday→intraday, overnight→overnight) 0.22–0.45 % per half-day gross, opposite-period reversal −0.25 % / −0.28 %, persisting 20 business days; intraday momentum grew from ≈ 0.20 % to 0.60 % per half-day and overnight momentum from 0.10 % to 1.00 % over the sample; strongest in options "costlier to arbitrage". Authors: "our analysis focuses on gross option returns … Even if our strategy's alpha is zero after costs". At single-name round trips of 7–8 % of premium this is not tradeable; on SPY (1 % round trip) a 0.2–0.5 % half-day signal is below cost. Not proposed.

## 3. Delta-hedged option returns and stock characteristics (the cross-section)

- Goyal & Saretto, "Cross-section of option returns and volatility", *JFE* 94(2), 2009, 310–326 [PR] — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X09001251; March 2008 draft read here — http://www.ruf.rice.edu/~jgsfss/goyal_041808.pdf. Sort on HV − IV (one-month ATM); decile straddle portfolios held to expiry; draft Table 3: straddle decile returns "go from −9.9 % to 12.6 %"; long-short **22.5 % per month**, monthly SD 31.4 %, worst month −40.5 %, Sharpe 0.718/month; "robust to … option liquidity characteristics". Costs (draft §5.2): with effective = quoted spread on options (plus TAQ stock costs at exercise) the long-short straddle return falls "from 22.5 % to 7.5 % per month"; liquid-option half (low spreads) 18.2 % → 9.4 % (significant), illiquid half 27.7 % → 5.9 % (insignificant). The published abstract gives **3.9 % per month** at an effective spread equal to the quoted spread (the search summary of the ScienceDirect abstract; the published table was not retrieved). Note that Heston et al.'s Table 2 replication of the same sort over 1996–2019 (quintiles) finds the cheap-IV long leg at −1.86 %/month: the long side's positive return in the 1996–2006 deciles did not persist at quintile granularity over the longer sample.
- Cao & Han, "Cross section of option returns and idiosyncratic stock volatility", *JFE* 108(1), 2013, 231–249 [PR] — https://optionmetrics.com/research/j-cao-and-b-han-cross-section-of-option-returns-and-idiosyncratic-stock-volatility/. Abstract: delta-hedged equity option returns "decrease monotonically" in the underlying's idiosyncratic volatility; limits-to-arbitrage proxies explain ≈ 40 % of the relation. Magnitudes from CXO's summary [SEC] — https://www.cxoadvisory.com/volatility-effects/extracting-a-volatility-premium-with-equity-options: ≈ 160,000 ATM delta-hedged positions on > 5,000 stocks, 1996–2006, held to maturity; delta-hedged calls lose 0.49 % of stock value (4.32 % of call value) on average; long low-IVOL / short high-IVOL delta-hedged calls ≈ 2 % per month. The profit is again in the short leg (high-IVOL options are the ones that lose).
- Zhan, Han, Cao & Tong, "Option Return Predictability", *RFS* 35(3), 2022, 1394–1442 [PR] — https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v35y2022i3p1394-1442..html; April 2016 draft read here [WP] — https://abfer.org/media/abfer-events-2016/annual-conference/investment-finance/226-option-return-predictability.pdf. Published abstract: expected returns to *writing* delta-hedged calls are negatively related to stock price, profit margin, profitability and positively to cash holding, cash-flow variance, share issuance, external financing, distress risk and analyst dispersion; "annual Sharpe ratio above two and remain profitable after transaction costs"; two option factors explain the profits. Draft: 204 months, 792 stocks/month, mean quoted spread of the ATM options ≈ 20 % of mid (Table 1); example ISSUE spread 1.46 %/month at the midpoint → 0.82 % at 75 % of quoted → 0.62 % at 100 %; "seven out of twelve" survive the full quoted spread. The implementable side is *writing* calls on the characteristic-sorted names, i.e. short naked calls.
- Bali, Beckmeyer, Mörke & Weigert, "Option Return Predictability with Machine Learning and Big Data", *RFS* 36(9), 2023, 3548–3602 [PR]; CFR WP 21-08 (Aug 2021) read here [WP] — https://www.cfr-cologne.de/download/workingpaper/cfr-21-08.pdf. 12 M delta-hedged option-month observations, 1996–2020, 1,747 optionable stocks/month, 265 characteristics; nonlinear ensemble long-short **2.63 % per month, Sharpe 2.06** at the midpoint; **1.33 % (t 10.97) at 15 % of the quoted spread, 0.47 % (t 4.41) at 25 %**, Sharpe 1.17/0.43; "short-term options … survive 25 % effective spreads", OTM short-term puts survive 50 %, long-dated options "turn insignificant or even negative" at 25 %; authors note Muravyev–Pearson's effective spreads are lower than OptionMetrics quoted spreads. Nothing is reported at 100 % of the quoted spread, which is the retail marketable-order case; by linear extrapolation of the 0 → 15 → 25 % path (2.63 → 1.33 → 0.47) the 100 % case is well below zero.
- Vasquez, "Equity Volatility Term Structures and the Cross Section of Option Returns", *JFQA* 52(6), 2017, 2727–2754 [PR] — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/equity-volatility-term-structures-and-the-cross-section-of-option-returns/F0A40E99FD2458367DD9A56A89783D38. Straddles on high-slope (long-dated IV ≫ one-month IV) firms outperform low-slope; sample January 1996–June 2007 for the working paper per Alpha Architect [SEC]. Heston et al. replicate the sort on 1996–2019: high-minus-low **+7.17 %/month (t 7.92)** gross, high quintile −2.78 % (t −1.69). Costs not retrieved from the published paper.
- Christoffersen, Goyenko, Jacobs & Karoui, "Illiquidity Premia in the Equity Options Market", *RFS* 31(3), 2018 [PR] — https://research.cbs.dk/en/publications/illiquidity-premia-in-the-equity-options-market-2/; CREATES 2013-48 draft read here. Published abstract (per search summary): risk-adjusted return spread of illiquid over liquid equity options 3.4 % per day for ATM calls, 2.5 % for ATM puts; draft: S&P 500 firms, quoted spreads used as effective-spread proxy (effective/quoted 0.8–1 for these names), net returns computed from bid and ask. The premium *is* the spread; a marketable-order account cannot harvest it.
- Hu & Jacobs, "Volatility and Expected Option Returns", *JFQA* 55(3), 2020, 1025–1060 [PR] — https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v55y2020i3p1025-1060_10.html. Expected call (put) returns are decreasing (increasing) in underlying volatility; holds in the cross-section of equity options and is "not due to cross-sectional variation in expected stock returns". A mechanical leverage effect, relevant as a contract-selection rule for a long-option program (calls on low-vol names, puts on high-vol names, holding the stock view fixed).
- Ramachandran & Tayal, "Mispricing, short-sale constraints, and the cross-section of option returns", *JFE* 141(1), 2021, 297–321 [PR] — https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v141y2021i1p297-321.html. Monotonic relation between short-sale constraints and delta-hedged *put* returns on overpriced stocks; "no robust relation for either put options on underpriced stocks or call options".
- Gerchik, "The Common Source of Option Return Anomalies", SSRN 7458380, 2025/26 [WP; abstract page blocked (403) — content from the search summary only]: many delta-hedged predictors reduce to one effect; long-short portfolios "lose money mainly because they hold options on volatile stocks, which get repriced at each monthly roll" — i.e. the short leg again.

Verdict for 3: the cross-sectional literature is a literature about *writing* the expensive options. Three independent full-sample tables (Heston et al. Table 2, 1996–2019; BBMW's 0→25 % cost path; Goyal–Saretto's own 22.5 → 7.5/3.9 %) agree that (i) no long-only straddle or delta-hedged-call portfolio has a positive mean, (ii) the long-short survives only at effective spreads far below what a marketable retail order pays, and (iii) the survivors require naked short single-name options with margin that halves the return. **L3** below is therefore stated as a *negative filter* for Compass's long-option universe, not a lane.

**L3 (selection filter, testable now).** For any long single-name option Compass would buy, record at decision time: IV − HV (one-month ATM IV from the NBBO mid minus trailing 21-day realized), idiosyncratic volatility, IV term slope, stock price level, share issuance/dispersion where available. Hypothesis: contracts in the top quintile of IV − HV, top quintile of IVOL, lowest price quintile underperform the rest of the eligible set by ≥ 2 % of premium per month gross (Heston Table 2 spreads 6–10 %/month between extreme quintiles). Test on the S03 weekly cache (SPY/QQQ/IWM) only as a smoke test — the effect is a single-name effect — then on Tier A option EOD once pulled. Expected effect on a Compass lane: it removes contracts with the worst expected carry; it cannot turn a zero-information entry (S04/S06) positive. After-cost gain: the filter costs nothing; its value is bounded above by the gross spread of the excluded quintile, 2–10 % of premium per month.

## 4. Idiosyncratic volatility and lottery options

- Bali, Cakici & Whitelaw, "Maxing out: Stocks as lotteries and the cross-section of expected returns", *JFE* 99(2), 2011, 427–446 [PR] — https://www.nber.org/papers/w14804.pdf (NBER draft). Stocks: lowest-minus-highest MAX decile > 1 % per month raw and risk-adjusted, July 1962–December 2005, NYSE/AMEX/NASDAQ; survives size, B/M, momentum, reversal, liquidity, skewness controls. A stock result; retail-accessible as a stock short/avoid rule; the option version is Boyer–Vorkink and Byun–Kim.
- Boyer & Vorkink, "Stock Options as Lotteries", *Journal of Finance* 69(4), 2014, 1485–1527 [PR] — https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v69y2014i4p1485-1527.html. Ex-ante total skewness of individual equity options "has a strong negative relationship with average option returns … return gaps of roughly 10 % to 50 % per week" across skewness quintiles, risk-adjusted; attributed to intermediaries' compensation for unhedgeable risk. Working-paper details per CXO [SEC] — https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=13232: 1996–2009, eight expiration horizons × skewness quintiles, spreads up to ≈ 60 %/week, larger for puts and nearer expiry. Costs: the published abstract does not mention transaction costs; the effect size is so large that it survives any spread on the *short* side, but the short side is writing the most skewed (lowest-priced, highest-gamma) options.

Verdict for 4: the lottery literature is the mirror image of the owner's long-option program — retail buys the top-skew, short-dated, low-priced contracts and the studies say those contracts lose 10–50 % per week relative to the rest before costs. The short side is institutional in practice (naked writing of the highest-gamma names, worst tails, 50 %+ margin haircuts). **L4** is the long-only use: a hard exclusion.

**L4 (exclusion rule, testable now).** Exclude from any Compass contract set: (a) ask < $0.10 or premium < 1 % of spot for a ≥ 5-day expiration (the lottery end of the chain; H14 already leaves such legs to expire on the short side), (b) the top quintile of the option-level smirk (OTM-put IV − ATM-call IV; Xing–Zhang–Zhao's measure, computable per name per day from the chain), (c) the top quintile of MAX (largest daily return in the prior month, from stock EOD) and of IVOL. Expected: in Boyer–Vorkink's sorts the top-skewness quintile underperforms the bottom by 10–50 % per week gross; even if the effect is one-tenth of that now, excluding it is worth more than any entry signal measured so far (S04/S06 ≤ 1 bp). Data: stock EOD 2012-06 (UTP) / 2017-01 (CTA) and option chains from 2012-06; nothing to pull beyond what S07/S11 already need. The rule cannot create a positive lane; it is worth adopting as a default like H14/H17.

## 5. Option-implied information predicting stock returns

- Xing, Zhang & Zhao, "What Does the Individual Option Volatility Smirk Tell Us About Future Equity Returns?", *JFQA* 45(3), 2010, 641–662 [PR] — https://www.pbcsf.tsinghua.edu.cn/__local/A/24/EA/815CC33800CAA0F7D894E9292A1_6CC8B38B_27B25.pdf. Smirk = OTM-put IV − ATM-call IV; January 1996–December 2005, weekly; steepest-smirk quintile underperforms the flattest by 16 bp per week raw (9.19 %/yr, t −2.19); long-flat/short-steep FF3 alpha 21 bp per week (10.90 %/yr, t 2.93); persists ≥ 6 months; steep-smirk firms have the worst next-quarter earnings surprises. Stock returns; no option transaction costs apply to the stock trade.
- Cremers & Weinbaum, "Deviations from Put-Call Parity and Stock Return Predictability", *JFQA* 45(2), 2010, 335–367 [PR] — https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v45y2010i02p335-367_00.html. Call-minus-put IV spread; stocks with relatively expensive calls outperform those with expensive puts by **50 bp per week**; "the degree of predictability decreases over the sample period"; stronger when option liquidity is high and stock liquidity low; not driven by hard-to-borrow stocks (rebate rates). A European replication "found no evidence that our trading strategy can be profitable" (CBS thesis, search summary).
- An, Ang, Bali & Cakici, "The Joint Cross Section of Stocks and Options", *Journal of Finance* 69(5), 2014, 2279–2338 [PR]; NBER 19590 read here — https://www.nber.org/papers/w19590.pdf. 1996–2011, 1,261 → 2,312 stocks/month. Decile spread on past one-month change in ATM call IV (ΔCVOL): **1.09 % per month** raw (t 3.45), 1.38 % (t 5.85) controlling for ΔPVOL; ΔPVOL spread −0.42 % raw (t −2.03), −1.04 % (t −6.40) controlling for ΔCVOL; decays from 1.38 % to 0.63 % in month two and 0.34 % by month four. Stock returns.
- Goncalves-Pinto, Grundy, Hameed, van der Heijden & Zhu, "Why Do Option Prices Predict Stock Returns? The Role of Price Pressure in the Stock Market", *Management Science* 66(9), 2020, 3903–3926 [PR] — https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v66y2020i9p3903-3926.html. The gap between option-implied and traded stock prices (DOTS) earns 85 bp alpha on the day after formation, is "strongly related to return reversals, order imbalances, and transaction costs in stocks, and only weakly related to trading activity in options"; the IV-spread measures of the earlier papers "are direct transformations of this cross-market price gap". The predictability is a one-day *stock* reversal, not information diffusing from options; it decays within days.
- Related, already in registry: H2 (flow-following entries, UW aggregates, closed), H3 (per-strike bursts, closed). The smirk/IV-spread predictors are price-based, not flow-based, and are a different claim at a different horizon (weeks); not closed. S07/S08 screened price/volume triggers only.

Arithmetic for a long-option expression (why none is proposed as an option lane): the long leg of these sorts earns ≈ 10–25 bp per week over the universe (XZZ 10 bp/week flat-smirk portfolio excess; CW 50 bp/week is the long-short). A one-week ATM single-name call at 35 % IV costs ≈ 0.4 × 0.35 × √(5/252) ≈ 2.0 % of spot; C1 half-spread 3.5–4.1 % of that premium = 7–8 bp of spot per side, 14–16 bp round trip, plus theta to expiry; a 0.5-delta capture of 25 bp is 12 bp. A 30-DTE call held one week costs 4.0 % of spot in premium, 28–32 bp round trip, and ≈ 51 bp of theta for the same 12 bp capture. Negative in every configuration. In the stock the long leg clears a stock round trip (≈ 2–5 bp on liquid names) comfortably.

Expected: long-leg +10–25 bp/week gross in 1996–2011 samples, declining (CW's own finding; Goncalves-Pinto's reversal reading); below the A2 option hurdle (+30 bp lower bound at 5–21 sessions) and useful only as a stock-side or direction-filter result. Cost of the test: option EOD per name per session for ~1,100–1,500 names (the S11 pull covers a subsample).

## 6. Overnight versus intraday, weekends and holidays

- Jones & Shemesh, "Option Mispricing around Nontrading Periods", *Journal of Finance* 73(2), 2018, 861–900 [PR] — http://faculty.marshall.usc.edu/Christopher-Jones/pdf/jones_shemesh_2018_jf.pdf. Daily, January 4, 1996–August 28, 2014, S&P 500 constituents (main sample), dollar-open-interest weighted, closing mid-quotes. Table II: delta-hedged puts **+0.03 % per trading day vs −0.82 % per nontrading period** (difference −0.86 %, t −11.93); delta-hedged calls +0.03 % vs −0.48 % (−0.51 %, t −5.11); Monday-ending returns −0.85 % (puts), Tuesday −0.16 %, Wednesday +0.19 %, Thursday +0.25 %, Friday −0.23 %; "no evidence of any nontrading effect in underlying stock returns". Table III: 59 of 60 delta × maturity portfolios have lower nontrading returns, 54 significant; largest for 11–53-day, −0.10 > δ ≥ −0.25 puts (−2.73 %, t −10.21) and 0.01 < δ ≤ 0.10 calls (−4.36 %, t −5.56). Table IV: ask-to-bid and bid-to-ask returns give the same sign (−0.75 % / −1.00 % for hedged puts); options with relative spreads ≤ 5 %: hedged puts −0.63 % (t −9.24); S&P 500 Index options value-weighted: puts −1.07 % (t −4.56), calls −0.30 % (t −0.91), equal-weighted deep-OTM −4.71 % (t −4.70); regular weekends −0.79 % (n 851), midweek holidays −0.32 % (n 44, t −0.83), long weekends −0.71 % (n 122). Negative in every year. Mechanism: options priced in calendar time; the effect loads on gamma, not vega. Costs: "none of the calculations … factor in the effects of transaction costs … the returns are not feasible as a stand-alone trading strategy. Instead, they represent the additional return that would result from holding previously written options for another day"; "Since bid-ask spreads are often large, it is not feasible to capture the entire nontrading effect in a stand-alone trading strategy based on market orders"; "For an option buyer, the nontrading return is the benefit from delaying the purchase by one day."
- Muravyev & Ni, "Why do option returns change sign from day to night?", *JFE* 136(1), 2020, 219–238 [PR] — https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v136y2020i1p219-238.html. Abstract: S&P 500 delta-hedged option returns −0.7 % per day; close-to-open −1 % per day, open-to-close +0.3 %; "similar return pattern holds for all maturity and moneyness categories and equity options". Intraday bid-ask quotes January 2004–April 2013 and equity-option magnitudes (overnight −0.4 %, intraday +0.1 %) per CXO [SEC] — https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-options/intraday-versus-overnight-option-returns/. Terstegge, "Intermediary Option Pricing" (HHS/Michigan WP) attributes the positive intraday premium to options that do not trade at the open; excluding stale opening quotes "yields insignificant intraday risk premia" (search summary; primary not read).
- Papagelis & Dotsis, "The Variance Risk Premium Over Trading and Non-Trading Periods", SSRN 4954623 (2024); *Journal of Futures Markets* 2025 per Wiley listing [WP/PR; abstract page blocked]. Per the Harbourfront summary [SEC] — https://harbourfrontquant.substack.com/p/breaking-down-the-volatility-risk: VRP negative overnight, positive intraday (US, Europe, Asia implied-variance indices); a long-volatility position opened at the open and closed at the close "has tended to be profitable on most days, with Fridays as the exception". Not verified against the paper.
- Sheikh & Ronn (1994, *JF*) is the only earlier weekly-pattern study (30 stocks, 21 months); Jones–Shemesh say it is superseded.

Registry: H8 closed "session plays in options (ORB, gap fill, half-hour seasonality, 15:00 momentum)" on UW contract bars 2025-04 → 2026-10; S16a/b closed overnight *gap* trading. The weekend/overnight *decay* effect is a different claim (delta-hedged, calendar-time theta, 19-year sample) and is not in the registry.
