# L3 — stock and option characteristics as predictors of delta-hedged single-name option returns: evidence-quality refutation (2026-10-14)

Lens: evidence quality (is the cited effect real, replicated, not a data-mining artefact; checked against the primary text where it could be retrieved). Hypothesis under test: registry-sweep id **L3** as stated in `academic-anomalies.md` §3 and §11 — "HV−IV long-short straddles 22.5 %/month gross falling to 7.5 % (draft) or 3.9 % (published) at the full quoted spread (Goyal–Saretto); delta-hedged returns fall monotonically in idiosyncratic volatility (Cao–Han); price level, profitability, issuance, dispersion predict returns to writing delta-hedged calls, 7 of 12 survive 100 % of the quoted spread in the 2016 draft (Zhan et al.); ML ensemble 2.63 %/month at mid, 1.33 % at 15 % and 0.47 % at 25 % of the quoted spread (Bali–Beckmeyer–Mörke–Weigert). All profits sit in the short (expensive) leg; retail use is as a negative filter for long options." Proposition to refute: *a retail-accessible option edge that is plausibly positive after costs and testable with our data over a window long enough for our gates.*

## 0. Verdict

**Refuted as a retail-accessible edge; the hypothesis itself only ever claimed a filter, and the filter has no measurable benefit until a positive long-option lane exists, which nothing in the registry provides (S01–S16b).** Beyond that, four things found here reduce what the filter could be worth even in principle:

1. **The numbers in the hypothesis are a mixture of draft, secondary and misread figures.** The "3.9 % published" Goyal–Saretto figure is not in the published abstract (IDEAS and a second mirror of the JFE abstract contain no percentage at all; the ScienceDirect page returned 403) and no primary source for it was found; the only primary after-cost number is the 2008 draft's Table 8: 7.5 %/month (t 2.32) at an effective spread equal to the quoted spread, 1996–2005. Zhan et al.'s draft Table 8 has **eight** strategies, not twelve, and "seven out of eight" survive the full quoted spread (the lone failure is size); the "seven out of twelve" in the sweep note is a misreading of the draft's skewness sentence. Cao–Han's "≈ 2 %/month" is gross; the working-paper versions summarised by CXO [SEC] put it at 0.22 %/month and "not statistically reliable" at 100 % of the quoted spread. BBMW report nothing at 100 % of the quoted spread and state that at 50 % "the unconditional strategy fails to generate positive returns"; at 25 % the strategy's rolling returns were "zero or turn slightly negative" in 2016–2019.
2. **The long leg is negative in every sort, in every sample.** Heston et al.'s Table 2 Panel B (1996–2019, 282 months, quintiles): the best long-straddle cell for the IV−HV sort is −1.86 %/month (t −1.10), for the IV term spread −2.78 % (t −1.69), for idiosyncratic volatility −4.74 % (t −2.75); Goyal–Saretto's own 1996–2005 top decile is the only positive long cell in the literature and did not persist. Garcia-Ares–Muravyev (1996–2023, 18 anomalies, delta-hedged calls): the "good" decile of every characteristic earns +0.03 to +0.57 % per month of hedged-position capital, i.e. ≈ 0 to +5 % of option premium before costs, and that is the leg a long-option program would hold.
3. **Half of the predictability is two days of rollover price pressure, and in liquid names all of it.** Garcia-Ares & Muravyev (FMA 2025 [WP], the most recent and the only paper that decomposes the anomalies in time): the average of 18 anomalies is −0.64 %/month (t −7.77), of which −0.36 % (t −13.7) falls on the third-Friday expiration and the following Monday; outside those two days the average is −0.28 % and only 7 of 18 characteristics remain significant. Restricted to S&P 500 names the full-month average is −0.10 % (t −1.78) and ex-rollover +0.04 % (t 0.68): **zero**. The idiosyncratic-volatility and price-level sorts — the two the hypothesis names as filters — are among those that vanish ex-rollover (IVOL −0.21 %, t −1.92; −ln(price) −0.15 %, t −1.07, broad sample; +0.14 % and +0.10 % in S&P 500 names). Only Goyal–Saretto's IV−HV deviation keeps a significant ex-rollover spread (−1.51 %, t −8.92 broad; −0.47 %, t −3.77 S&P 500). The mechanism is covered-call writers' rollover selling absorbed by market makers; the profit is liquidity provision on those two days, a dealer role.
4. **Measurement critiques cut the magnitudes further.** Goyenko–Zhang: closing mid-quotes are biased and the closing spread is ≈ 50 bp wider than intraday; Duarte–Jones–Wang (JF 2024): zero-mean price noise biases measured option returns upward, most for the noisiest (cheap, illiquid, high-IVOL) options; Eberbach et al. (2025): the indirect bias of daily-rebalanced delta-hedged returns (the Cao–Han/BBMW construction) manufactures a spurious ≈ 3 %/month premium between low- and high-liquidity underlyings in a simulation with no true premium; Duarte–Jones–Khorram–Mo–Wang (RFS 2025, forthcoming): ex-post sample filters in published option-anomaly strategies inflate Sharpe ratios by an order of magnitude. None of these says the IV−HV effect is zero; together they say the sizes in the hypothesis are upper bounds.

What survives: a ranking of which single-name long options carry the richest implied volatility relative to realized (Goyal–Saretto's deviation), worth at most the ex-rollover gap between the expensive decile and the rest — ≈ 0.5 %/month of hedged capital ≈ 5 % of premium per month in the broad sample, ≈ 0.5 % of premium in S&P 500 names — on a one-month hold; on a Compass hold of minutes to three sessions it is a fraction of that, below the per-trade noise of a single-name option (§3). As an exclusion rule it costs nothing and may be adopted like H14/H17, but it is not an edge, it cannot be gated (no lane to measure it on), and its one local relative — S15's implied-vs-realized sort on index 0DTE straddles — had the wrong sign.

## 1. The claims as cited versus the primary texts

### 1.1 Goyal & Saretto, "Cross-section of option returns and volatility", *JFE* 94(2) 2009, 310–326 [PR]; March 2008 draft read [WP]

Primary: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X09001251 (403 today); published abstract confirmed at https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v94y2009i2p310-326.html and https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/357238 — **it contains no numbers**: "a zero-cost trading strategy that is long (short) in the portfolio with a large positive (negative) difference between these two volatility measures produces an economically and statistically significant average monthly return … robust to … option liquidity characteristics". Draft: http://www.ruf.rice.edu/~jgsfss/goyal_041808.pdf (also Purdue CIBER WP 55).

- Data: OptionMetrics closing bid–ask, 1996–2005; HV = standard deviation of daily returns over the trailing twelve months; IV = average of the one-month ATM call and put IVs; deciles on HV − IV; straddles and delta-hedged calls/puts bought at the closing midpoint and **held to expiration** (static hedge); the authors skip a day after formation so "our traders trade only based on the closing quotes on Tuesday" (fn. 22).
- Headline (§1 and Table 3): long-short straddle **22.5 %/month, Sharpe 0.718**, monthly SD 31.4 %; delta-hedged call and put 10−1 spreads 2.7 % and 2.6 % per month (Table 4/8, as fractions of the hedged position). Sub-periods: higher in 1996–2000 than 2001–2005.
- Costs (§5.2, Table 8): effective spread assumed at 50/75/100 % of the quoted spread, paid at initiation only, plus TAQ stock costs at exercise. Straddle 10−1: **0.225 (t 7.83) → 0.140 (4.27) → 0.107 (3.30) → 0.075 (2.32)**. Split by option bid–ask spread: liquid half 0.182 → 0.094 (t 2.26), illiquid half 0.277 → 0.059 (t 1.37). Delta-hedged calls 0.027 → 0.006 (t 1.32), puts 0.026 → 0.006 (t 1.44) at 100 %: "statistically insignificant returns of around 0.6 %". The authors' conclusion: "trading costs reduce the profits to our portfolios but do not eliminate them at reasonable estimates of effective spreads", with "reasonable" meaning the pre-penny effective/quoted ratios of 0.5–0.8 they cite.
- **The "3.9 % per month at the quoted spread" attributed to the published version is unverified.** It is not in the abstract; an extended search for it returned only the draft numbers (22.5 → 7.5; the 2006 draft's 15.0 → 5.3 at half the quoted spread) and a 2005 Saretto single-author paper in which "3.9 %" is a quintile forecast. It should be dropped from the hypothesis text unless the published Table 8 is read.
- Mechanism offered: mean-reversion of volatility mis-estimated by option prices ("large deviations of IV from HV are indicative of mis-estimation of mean reversion").

### 1.2 Cao & Han, "Cross section of option returns and idiosyncratic stock volatility", *JFE* 108(1) 2013, 231–249 [PR]

Primary text not retrieved (https://www-2.rotman.utoronto.ca/facbios/file/Han_JFE_published.pdf → 404; SSRN 1786607 → 403). Published abstract (https://optionmetrics.com/research/j-cao-and-b-han-cross-section-of-option-returns-and-idiosyncratic-stock-volatility/ ; https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v108y2013i1p231-249.html): "delta-hedged equity option return decreases monotonically with an increase in the idiosyncratic volatility of the underlying stock … consistent with market imperfections and constrained financial intermediaries … Dealers charge a higher premium for options on high idiosyncratic volatility stocks due to their higher arbitrage costs"; controlling for limits-to-arbitrage proxies "reduces the strength of the negative relation by about 40 %". Daily-rebalanced delta hedge (Bakshi–Kapadia gains scaled by ΔS − C), ≈ 1.5-month ATM options.

Magnitudes [SEC — CXO summaries of the July 2009 and September 2010 working-paper versions, https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=2210 and https://www.cxoadvisory.com/volatility-effects/extracting-a-volatility-premium-with-equity-options]: ≈ 160,000 (2009 version, 1996–2006) / ≈ 200,000 (2010 version, 1996-01 → 2009-10) ATM delta-hedged positions on > 5,000 stocks; delta-hedged calls lose 0.49 % (0.48 %) of stock value, 4.32 % (4.89 %) of call value on average; low-minus-high volatility covered-call spread **2.33 %/month** gross; at effective spreads of 50/75/100 % of the quoted spread **1.25 % / 0.73 % / 0.22 %**, the last "not statistically reliable"; the 2010 version: frictions reduce profitability "from about 1 % per month to approximately zero"; CXO's reading: "only traders with low trading frictions, such as option market makers, can reliably capture the high-volatility premium". These are the working-paper numbers; the published table was not read.

### 1.3 Zhan, Han, Cao & Tong, "Option Return Predictability", *RFS* 35(3) 2022, 1394–1442 [PR]; April 2016 draft read [WP]

Published abstract (https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v35y2022i3p1394-1442..html): expected returns to writing delta-hedged calls are negatively related to stock price, profit margin and profitability and positively to cash holding, cash-flow variance, share issuance, external financing, distress risk and analyst dispersion; "Our option portfolio strategies have annual Sharpe ratio above two and remain profitable after transaction costs"; "Their profits can be explained by two option factors". Draft: https://abfer.org/media/abfer-events-2016/annual-conference/investment-finance/226-option-return-predictability.pdf (a February 2016 version is at https://www.mcgill.ca/desautels/files/desautels/option_return_predictability_0.pdf).

- Sample (draft §2): 159,902 option-month observations, 204 months 1996-01 → 2012-12, 792 stocks/month on average, 40 % of CRSP market cap, options ≈ 50 calendar days to maturity at the month-end formation; Table 1: **mean quoted bid–ask spread of the ATM options 19.3 % of mid (calls), 20.5 % (puts); median 15.6 / 16.4 %**; mean delta-hedged gain to maturity −1.26 % of (ΔS − C), SD 7.7 %.
- Return definition: "delta-hedged gain / (ΔS − C)" — per dollar of the *hedged position's* capital, which is dominated by the stock leg. For a $100 stock at 35 % IV the 50-DTE ATM call is ≈ $5.2 and ΔS − C ≈ $48 per share, so 1 % of hedged capital ≈ 9 % of option premium [LOCAL].
- Costs (draft §4.1, Table 8, equal-weighted 10−1 spreads to delta-neutral *call writing*, effective = 0/10/25/50/75/100 % of the quoted spread): IVOL 3.92 → 1.61 (t 10.97); CH 1.99 → 1.04; RET(−1,0) 1.28 → 0.73; ISSUE 1.46 → 0.62 (t 6.17); PROFIT 2.39 → 0.56; DISP 2.03 → 0.51; RET(−12,−2) 1.58 → 0.42; −ln(ME) 3.79 → **−0.22 (t −0.95)**. Text: "even if the effective option spread is as large as 100 % of the quoted spread, **seven out of eight** of our option strategies still deliver positive average returns that are statistically significant (the lone exception is the strategy based on stock size)". The "seven out of twelve" in the sweep note is a misreading of "Seven out of twelve spreads have positive skewness" (draft §3). Twelve characteristics are tested in the regressions (Table 3/4); eight are carried to the cost table.
- Table 9: the spreads are 1.3–1.6 %/month larger in the illiquid, low-priced, low-institutional-ownership, low-coverage quintiles ("the profits of option strategies are difficult to arbitrage amongst stocks with lower liquidity, price level, institutional ownership, or analyst coverage"). The implementable side is naked delta-hedged call writing on ≈ 80 names per decile, rebalanced monthly.

## 2. Replications, extensions and critiques (evidence quality)

- **Gross effects replicate across teams, samples and return definitions.** Horenstein, Vasquez & Xiao, "Common Factors in Equity Option Returns", *RFS* 39(3) 2026, 835–874 [PR; abstract only, via the Sydney seminar page and the Miami record — PDF not retrieved]: a four-factor model for delta-hedged equity option returns whose three characteristic factors are size, idiosyncratic volatility and the IV−HV gap, "not spanned by the factors in equity returns". Cao, Vasquez, Xiao & Zhan, "Volatility Uncertainty and the Cross-Section of Option Returns", Dec 2019 [WP] (https://topcat.aeaweb.org/conference/2020/preliminary/paper/k6denSQi; OptionMetrics 1996-01 → 2016-04): the volatility-of-volatility effect "remain[s] negative and significant after controlling for" IV−HV, the term-structure slope and IVOL, i.e. those three are live controls through 2016. Vasquez & Xiao, "Default Risk and Option Returns" 2019 [WP] (https://www.hec.ca/finance/Fichier/Xiao2019.pdf; 1996–2016): "For nine out of ten anomalies, the long-short option returns are higher for high default risk" firms. Garcia-Ares & Muravyev (below) reproduce all 18 characteristics on 1996-01 → 2023-08 with the Duarte et al. look-ahead correction. The sweep's "data-mining artefact" question is answered: the sorts are single-variable, t-statistics of 5–10 on 200–330 months, replicated by at least four independent teams, with mechanisms (volatility mean-reversion, dealer inventory/limits to arbitrage, retail demand for low-priced options).
- **Garcia-Ares & Muravyev, "Option returns: a tale of the expiration rollover day", 20 June 2025 [WP], FMA Derivatives & Volatility 2025 — https://www.fma.org/assets/docs/Derivatives2025/GarciaAres.pdf** (earlier SSRN 4595840, "Equity Option Return Predictability and Expiration Days"; discussants include Duarte and Zhdanov). OptionMetrics 1996-01 → 2023-08, daily-rebalanced delta-hedged calls ≈ 50 → 20 DTE, calendar-month returns, Duarte et al. (2022) look-ahead correction; Cboe open/close customer imbalances 2005-05 → 2021-12. Average calendar-month delta-hedged call return −0.20 %; over expiration Friday + the following Monday **−0.43 %**, over the rest of the month **+0.19 %**, both significant; customer imbalance in non-expiring options +1 % on a normal day, **−12 % on the post-expiration Monday**. Table 4 (18 anomalies, decile 10 − 1, per month of hedged capital; full month / ex-rollover / rollover): average **−0.64 (t −7.77) / −0.28 (−3.90) / −0.36 (−13.69)**; −VOL DEVIATION (Goyal–Saretto) −1.76 (−9.30) / **−1.51 (−8.92)** / −0.25; IVOL −0.77 (−6.55) / **−0.21 (−1.92)** / −0.57 (−15.35); −ln(PRICE) −0.71 (−4.67) / **−0.15 (−1.07)** / −0.56; −IV TERM −0.69 / −0.47 (−4.46) / −0.22; DISP −0.24 / +0.04 / −0.28; ISSUE (1 y) −0.45 / −0.12 (−1.63) / −0.33; −PROFIT −0.26 / −0.02 / −0.24; ln(ME) −0.37 / +0.04 / −0.40; CALL IV ATM −1.85 / −0.98 / −0.87. Decile 1 (the "good" side, the one a long program would hold) ex-rollover: +0.35 to +0.95 %, all t > 3; decile 10 ex-rollover: VOL DEVIATION −0.55 (t −3.63), CALL IV −0.47, IVOL +0.15 (t 0.96), PRICE +0.21 (t 1.24). Table 5 (S&P 500 members only, "the most actively traded options and lowest transaction costs"): average **−0.10 (t −1.78) / +0.04 (0.68) / −0.14 (−11.20)**; VOL DEVIATION −0.50 / **−0.47 (−3.77)** / −0.03; IVOL −0.14 (−1.37) / +0.14 / −0.28; −ln(PRICE) −0.09 / +0.10 / −0.20; −IV TERM −0.34 (−2.55) / −0.17 (−1.32) / −0.17; "only 4 anomalies are significant for the full month, 16 out of 18 … during the expiration window". Unreported result: selling the top-decile call delta-hedged from Thursday's close before expiration to Monday's close "remains profitable … even after accounting for standard option transaction costs". Reading: outside the two rollover days the IVOL, price-level, issuance, profitability, dispersion and size effects are not distinguishable from zero in the broad sample and are zero or positive in liquid names; the Goyal–Saretto deviation and the ATM IV level are the survivors.
- **Measurement.** Goyenko & Zhang, May 2020 [WP] (https://ruslangoyenko.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Option-Returns-MAY-24-2020.pdf): closing mid-quotes are pushed by end-of-day order imbalance and mean-revert the next morning; closing quoted spreads jump ≈ 50 bp; "continuing using end-of-day closing prices to compute option returns will lead to false discoveries of new anomalies"; 10 am quotes give equity and SPX delta-hedged returns of −1.3 % / −1.6 % per day-equivalent that are stable across 2011–2014 and 2015–2018 while closing-quote returns flip sign. Duarte, Jones & Wang, "Very Noisy Option Prices and Inference Regarding the Volatility Risk Premium", *JF* 2024, DOI 10.1111/jofi.13365 [PR; January 2022 draft read, http://faculty.marshall.usc.edu/Christopher-Jones/pdf/duarte_jones_wang_2022.pdf]: zero-mean measurement error in option and stock prices biases average option returns upward (Blume–Stambaugh), with an indirect bias through hedge ratios that is "relatively large in some other cases such as straddles"; after correction the volatility risk premium in heavily traded equity options is ≈ −5 bp/day, like SPX; "without bias corrections, the FM regressions indicate that volatility is not negatively priced in individual equity options". Eberbach, Molnar, Schuster & Uhrig-Homburg, "Asset Pricing Results in Options Markets: True, Spurious, or Overlooked?", Oct 2025 [WP] (https://www.fma.org/assets/docs/Derivatives2025/Molnar.pdf): in a controlled simulation with no true premium, daily or intraday rebalancing of the delta hedge "can inflate delta-hedged option returns by nearly 100 bp per month" and "generates a spurious premium of 3 % per month for delta-hedged calls on stocks with low liquidity compared to those on stocks with high liquidity"; "Existing bias adjustments fail to correct this distortion"; they name Cao–Han (2013), Bali et al. (2023) and Vasquez–Xiao as daily-rebalanced measures and Goyal–Saretto and Zhan et al. as static. Since IVOL, price level and Amihud sorts are liquidity sorts, part of their delta-hedged spread is this bias; the static Goyal–Saretto straddle sort is not exposed to it. Duarte, Jones, Khorram, Mo & Wang, "Too Good to Be True: Look-ahead Bias in Empirical Options Research", *RFS* forthcoming (posted 16 June 2025) [PR; abstract not retrieved — SSRN 4590083 403; description from the Rice Business write-up https://www.business.rice.edu/wisdom/when-research-promises-impossible-investors-pay-price and CXO [SEC]]: two published option strategies (one on stock characteristics, 2012-01 → 2020-06; one on options-market liquidity provision, 1996-01 → 2016-04) were replicated and then recast with only information available before trading; one strategy's "daily mean returns of more than 3 %" came from a liquidity filter using the contract's full-day average price to select contracts at the start of that day; a citing paper describes Sharpe ratios inflated "from 0.5 to above 5" by ex-post filters. Garcia-Ares–Muravyev apply the correction and still find the 18 anomalies at −0.64 %/month, so the effects are not entirely look-ahead; the correction is why their magnitudes are a third of Zhan's.
- **Who pays.** Hollstein & Wese Simen, "How do Investors Trade Option Anomalies?", 21 Mar 2025 [WP; abstract only, https://optionmetrics.com/research/f-hollstein-c-wese-simen-how-do-investors-trade-option-anomalies ; SSRN 5034623 403]: "customers are overwhelmingly on the wrong side of anomalies. Market makers are the main beneficiaries … end-user demand also emerges as a likely driver of several anomalies … they mainly seek outright rather than delta-hedged option exposure". Eisdorfer, Goyal, Zhdanov (with Boulatov in the 2020 SFI version), "Cheap Options Are Expensive" / "Limited Attention and Option Prices", SFI 20-64 [WP; abstract https://www.sfi.ch/en/publications/n-20-64-cheap-options-are-expensive]: "delta-hedged options on low-priced stocks underperform those on high-priced stocks by 0.63 % per week for calls and 0.36 % for puts", attributed to retail "(partial) inattention to the underlying stock prices"; 1996–2017 per the RePEc listing [SEC]. The price-level characteristic in L3 is therefore a retail-demand effect: a retail account is the demand.
- **Not found after extended search:** any post-publication decay study for these option sorts (the McLean–Pontiff literature is stocks); any replication at intraday quotes; any after-cost replication at 100 % of the quoted spread by a third party on data after 2012. The CXO, Alpha Architect, Harbourfront and Quantpedia pages are [SEC] summaries of the papers above and add no numbers beyond those marked.

Evidence-quality reading: peer-reviewed, replicated, large-t gross effects with stated mechanisms — not data mining. But (i) the magnitudes in the hypothesis are draft or secondary, one is unverified and one is miscounted; (ii) the one in-sample decomposition that exists (1996–2023) attributes half of the average anomaly, and all of it in liquid names, to two rollover days; (iii) the IVOL and price-level sorts — the filters named — are the ones that do not survive ex-rollover, while the Goyal–Saretto deviation does; (iv) the measurement literature says the daily-rebalanced magnitudes are upper bounds.

## 3. What a 2026 retail account would actually face [LOCAL arithmetic on the papers' numbers and C1]

- **Cost column.** GATE-v2 prices marketable orders at the NBBO; C1 forbids mid fills. Goyal–Saretto's own 100 %-of-quoted row is the retail column: 7.5 %/month (t 2.32) on 1996–2005 straddles, 9.4 % (t 2.26) in the liquid half, 0.6 % (t 1.3–1.4) for delta-hedged calls/puts. Zhan's 100 % column: 0.42–1.61 %/month of hedged capital ≈ 4–15 % of option premium, for *writing* calls on ≈ 80 names per decile with the stock hedge. Cao–Han's WP: 0.22 %, not reliable [SEC]. BBMW: unreported; zero at 50 %. The papers' quoted spreads (mean 19–20 % of mid, median 15.6 %) are the same order as the S11 closing-quote measurement (median half-spread 11.1 % near the money at 8–30 DTE, p25 5.0 %, p75 28.6 %; `execution-costs.md` §2.2), so the papers' 100 % column is roughly what a close-executed single-name program pays today; the 10:00 C1 medians (3.5–4.1 % half-spread on the mixed book, 0.8–1.3 % on mega-caps) are about half of it. No paper reports a column between 100 % of the quoted spread and the Muravyev–Pearson execution-timing fractions.
- **The only positive expectation is the short leg**, and in Garcia-Ares–Muravyev it is positive on two days a month: a dealer-style liquidity-provision trade (sell delta-hedged calls into the rollover, hold Thursday → Monday). Naked single-name writing is the top approval tier (Schwab $25,000 minimum equity for uncovered equity options; TradeStation Level 5 — `L2-option-momentum-refutation.md` §3 [PRAC]); Reg-T margin for a short equity straddle is ≈ 3.5× its value (FINRA 4210, `execution-costs.md` §7 [EX]); Heston et al.'s single-straddle SD of 80.6 %/month means a ten-name leg carries ≈ 26 %/month of idiosyncratic noise against spreads of 2–10 %/month. Institutional-only in practice.
- **The long leg, the Compass scope.** Every long cell is negative gross (§1.6). The best is the low-IV−HV quintile at −1.86 %/month; after a single-name round trip at the 10:00 C1 half-spread (two legs × two sides × 3.5–4.1 % ≈ 7–8 % of premium) it is ≈ −9 to −10 %/month; at the closing median it is destroyed. So "retail use as a negative filter" is the only use, and the hypothesis says so.
- **What the filter is worth, bounded.** Monthly delta-hedged holds (Garcia-Ares–Muravyev Table 4, ex-rollover, broad sample): the expensive-IV decile loses 0.55 % of hedged capital per month ≈ 5 % of premium; the high-IVOL and low-price deciles lose nothing ex-rollover (+0.15 %, +0.21 %, t ≈ 1). In S&P 500 names the expensive-IV decile loses 0.02 % ex-rollover (≈ 0.2 % of premium) and the IVOL/price deciles gain. A Compass position is not a one-month delta-hedged hold: S01/S09 are 60-minute 0DTE holds, S03 is ≤ 3 sessions on 1–7 DTE weeklies, S11/A5.2 is ≤ 21 sessions on 30–45 DTE. The volatility-carry component of a raw long-option P&L scales with the fraction of the option's life held; at ≤ 3 sessions of a weekly the IV−HV content is ≤ ≈ 1.5 % of premium per trade at the broad-sample size and ≈ 0.1 % at the S&P 500 size, against a per-trade SD of ≈ 30 % of premium (80.6 %/month scaled by √(3/21)); a one-sided 95 % lower bound above zero at 1.5 % needs ≈ 1,100 trades per quintile cell, at 0.5 % ≈ 10,000 [LOCAL]. The S03 cache produced 818 fills in nine years on three ETFs. The filter's benefit cannot be measured on any existing Compass lane, and it has nothing to improve: S01/S02/S03/S09 entries carry no information, so excluding a quintile of them changes the mean of zero by the quintile's carry, which is the number above.
- **Direction of the local evidence.** The one registry test of an implied-versus-realized sort on an option return is S15 (SPY/QQQ 0DTE ATM straddles at 10:00, n 1,941): rich implied (≥ 1.25× trailing realized) → −$41.10 [−86.33, −2.39] for the *short* straddle, i.e. the rich-IV contracts were the ones whose realized move was largest; the sign is the opposite of Goyal–Saretto's at the one-day horizon on index products. It does not test L3 (single names, one-month tenor) but it is the only local data point and it is not supportive.
- **A timing by-product worth one line.** Garcia-Ares–Muravyev's −12 % customer imbalance and −0.43 % two-day return on non-expiring single-name options over the third Friday/Monday imply that a long single-name option opened before that window pays the rollover pressure and one opened at Monday's close receives it. This is an entry-timing rule of the L6/L7 kind, measurable on the S11 event chains at the close (the losses are on closing marks), not a lane.

## 4. Testability with our data and the gate [LOCAL]

- **Signal inputs.** One-month ATM IV needs a chain with a ≈ 30-DTE expiration per name per date. The S03 cache holds SPY/QQQ/IWM nearest-Friday weeklies only (≤ 7 DTE; `execution-costs.md` §2.2: "Nothing longer than 7 DTE is cached for the index products"), so the proposed "S03 smoke test" cannot compute the paper's signal, has no cross-section (three index ETFs), and is a time-series VRP sort of the S15 kind, not L3. The S11 event cache (`fetch_cache/eod_exp`: 415 roots, 980 event-date__expiration files, 2024-01-02 → 2026-10-09, one 30–45-DTE expiration per event, closing NBBO) can give IV−HV and price level for event-selected names on event dates only; no second expiration is cached, so the IV term slope is absent. Trailing 21-day realized volatility and price level: stock EOD lake 2023 → 2026 (2022 downloading; 2012 → as years land). Idiosyncratic volatility needs a market series: SPY EOD from 2017-01 (CTA floor) or SPY 1-minute bars from 2012-06. Share issuance, profitability, dispersion: no fundamentals or analyst data in the lake.
- **The real test.** A monthly cross-section on Tier A (≈ 150 roots) 2012-06 → 2026-10 needs ≈ 150 × 160 option-EOD requests (one per root-month-expiration) ≈ 24,000 requests; at the throttle observed in the S11 pull (≈ 0.17 requests/s) that is ≈ 39 hours of the single login, which the stock-EOD backfill holds (DATA-3). Feasible, not cached.
- **Power for the gross sort** (monthly straddles, one-month hold, closing or 10:00 marks): Heston's IV−HV spread has SD ≈ 18 %/month at ≈ 270 names per quintile; at 30 names per quintile ≈ 55 %/month; a one-sided 95 % lower bound above zero at a 10 %/month gross spread needs ≈ 82 months; the 2012–2026 window has ≈ 170. So the *gross* Goyal–Saretto sort is reproducible on Tier A at 10:00 marks — a data-quality replication, worth doing once if Compass ever holds single-name options for weeks, and the first such test on intraday quotes. It is not an edge test: GATE-v2's statistical gate applies to a lane's net expectancy, and the sort's net long-only expectancy is negative in every paper.
- **Power for the filter on a Compass lane**: §3 — ≈ 1,100–10,000 trades per cell at the effect sizes the ex-rollover tables support, on lanes that do not exist.

**Decision.** Refuted as an edge. Record L3 as: (a) a cost-free exclusion rule that may be adopted by default for any future single-name long-option lane — exclude the top quintile of one-month ATM IV minus trailing realized volatility at decision time (the one characteristic that survives ex-rollover), and treat IVOL and price level as covered by L4's lottery exclusion rather than as separate filters; (b) a gross replication of the Goyal–Saretto sort on Tier A at 10:00 marks, queued behind the pulls Compass already needs, as a data-quality exercise; (c) no registry id until (b) runs or a lane exists to filter. What would change the verdict: a Tier A replication showing the expensive-IV quintile of *weekly* single-name options underperforming the rest by more than the per-trade noise over ≤ 3-session holds with a positive day-clustered lower bound — nothing in the literature measures that tenor.
